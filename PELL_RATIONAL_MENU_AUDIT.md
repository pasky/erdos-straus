A menu here is a **fixed finite list of triples of rational functions** on `S: x²−P y²=−1` over `ℚ`. Success means that at an ordinary prime `p` there exists an integral Pell witness, and all three formulas on a branch are defined and give a positive integral ES triple. The witness may be chosen *after* seeing `p`. No extra unrestricted gcd, floor, factorization or variable-index Pell operation is a rational function. Statements below about counting successes do not count ordinary ES solutions outside the menu.

**1. Polynomial witness bound (unconditional).** Put `s=√P`, `t=x+sy`; then `x=(t−t⁻¹)/2`, `y=(t+t⁻¹)/(2s)`. The involution sends `(s,t)↦(−s,−1/t)`. In particular a branch slot independent of `t` belongs to `ℚ(P)`. Take positive `x,y` so `t>1` (for other sign conventions allow the four sign-changed formulas and use `|x|+√p|y|`). For each fixed `t`-dependent slot there is an effectively obtainable constant `B` such that at every successful witness, `t≤p^B` for all sufficiently large `p`.

Indeed write the slot `A(s,t)/D(s,t)` with integer polynomial coefficients. A nonzero coefficient minor between `A,D` stays nonzero at `s=√p` except at finitely many `p`. For slot value `m` we have `1≤m≤2p⁴` (sort the triple; `x₁≤3p/4`, `x₂≤2px₁`, `x₃≤px₁x₂`). Thus `A(√p,T)−mD(√p,T)` is a **nonzero** polynomial with root `t`. Every coefficient and its conjugate have magnitude `≪p^C` uniformly in `m`; each *nonzero* coefficient has magnitude `≫p^(−C)` because its quadratic norm is a nonzero integer. Cauchy's root bound proves the claim, even if the original top coefficient vanishes. This one test handles finite limits, poles, and removable leading terms without having to separate an exact integer limit from its approximants. The bound is for a nonconstant slot, not for every witness at a prime that can be solved by some constant branch.

For a triple of fiber constants, infinitely many successes force each rational function `f(P)` to be polynomial: divide into a rational polynomial (bounded coefficient denominator) plus a proper rational remainder tending to zero. Integer values at unbounded integers force the remainder to vanish. The cleared ES identity must also hold identically. These branches are **not** sparse over all primes; e.g. ordinary solvable congruences occur. Rather, for a finite menu choose a common `M` divisible by 840 and all coefficient denominators of the surviving polynomial triples. No such triple succeeds at any sufficiently large prime `p≡1 mod M`: integrality at one such `p` implies integrality throughout `1 mod M`; eventual positivity and the primitive square-class obstruction in `LOGIC_SPLIT_SQUARE_TEST.md` give the contradiction. Eventual positivity here follows either from infinitely many positive successes or from the positive sector of the identity being clopen on `P>0`.

**2. What can be counted without Hooley?** For a nonconstant branch the successful positive Pell witness `t²=(2x²+1)+2xy√p` satisfies `1<t²≤p^(2B)`. In Diao, [arXiv:2408.03774v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03774), §1.2 eqs. (1.6)–(1.7), `S(X,α)` counts *positive norm-one solutions*, not only fundamental units, up to `d^(1/2+α)` over nonsquare `2≤d≤X`. Hence (1.7) for a sufficiently large fixed `α` gives `O_B(√X log² X)` successful primes from these branches, in particular on `1 mod M`. It is conjectural for general `B`. (z74 Opus xreview fix, explicit conditional statement and source check.) In Diao's notation `S(x,α)=#{η_d: 2≤d≤x, ε_d≤η_d≤d^(1/2+α)}`; (1.6) is Hooley's *theorem* only for `0<α≤1/2`, which is useless here because `t²>p`; (1.7) for `α>1/2` is a consequence of **Hooley's Conjecture 1** (C. Hooley, *On the Pellian equation and the class number of indefinite binary quadratic forms*, J. reine angew. Math. 353 (1984) 98–131). Hence: **CONDITIONAL on Hooley's Conjecture 1 at `α=2B−1/2`**, every fixed finite rational menu on one integral witness has `O(√X log²X)` successful primes `≤X` from its `t`-dependent branches; with §1 for the fiber-constant branches it misses relative density one of the primes `≡1 mod M`. This general statement must not be quoted as unconditional. An `o(X)` exceptional bound over all discriminants alone would not imply `o(X/log X)` over primes.

A sharper elementary low-height range uses norm **minus one directly**. For `X/2<p≤X`, suppose `t≤C p^B` with fixed `B≥1/2`. Then `y≪X^(B−1/2)=:O(Y)` and `x≤√X y`. For fixed `y` we need `x²≡−1 mod y²`, with at most `2^ω(y)` roots by prime powers/CRT (no roots at `4|y²`). Thus the number of pairs, ignoring primality, is

`≪ Σ_{y≪Y} 2^ω(y) (1+√X/y) ≪ Y log(2Y)+√X log²(2Y)`.

The second inequality uses `2^ω≤τ` and the elementary divisor sums. We get the square-root-log bound for `B≤1`, and a power saving relative to the primes whenever `B<3/2`. For a low-degree example, a designated **least** ES denominator `a x+b y+c` with fixed rational constants and `a≠0` forces `t≪p` since `x/t→1/2`, `y/t=O(p^(−1/2))` and the least denominator is `≤3p/4`. A proper rational slot `P+1/y` at *any* position forces `y=1` outright. Low `t`-degree *by itself* is not an explicit low-height bound: coefficients can have arbitrarily high degree in `P`, and a slot not known to be least cannot be bounded by `3p/4`.

**3. A curve bound, independent of the height exponent (unconditional, Siegel).** On any fixed proper algebraic curve of `S`, the number of distinct primes `p≤X` occurring at integral triples `(p,x,y)` is `O_curve(√X)`, possibly ineffective. Here are the details to avoid assuming that Siegel gives a uniform bound on the varying Pell fibers. Take irreducible affine components of the fixed locus, their normalizations and smooth projective completions. Vertical components give only finitely many `p`. Singularities and geometrically reducible rational components contribute at most finitely many rational points unless on a geometrically integral rational component (distinct conjugate components meet in finitely many points). Ignore these fixed exceptions. If there are infinitely many affine integral points, Siegel's integral-point theorem requires genus zero and at most two geometric points at infinity. A rational point identifies the projective normalization with `ℙ¹`. Evaluating any regular function on the affine normalization at nonsingular integral input coordinates gives a number of bounded denominator in its field of definition: it is integral over the affine coordinate ring, so multiply by a fixed integer clearing the coefficients of a monic dependence equation. This permits use of integral points on the normalization without assuming integral lifts on an arbitrary chosen integral model.

* One point at infinity is rational. Use a rational parameter `z` with its only pole there. Its values have bounded denominator and `P,x,y` are polynomials in `z`. Since `P` is nonconstant and `x²+1=P y²`, degree comparison gives `deg P=2(deg x−deg y)≥2`. There are `O(√X)` such parameter values at `0<P≤X`.
* Two points at infinity split over a field `K` of degree ≤2. Choose a parameter `z` over `K` with zero and pole there. Both `z` and `1/z` have bounded denominators, so the fractional ideals `(z)` at integral points range over a **finite** set (not an unrestricted set of S-units). Each nonempty ideal class of values is a coset of ordinary units of `O_K`, of rank at most one. The nonconstant rational map `P=f(z)` gives `h(P)=deg(f)h(z)+O_f(1)` for the absolute logarithmic height (homogenize a coprime pair and use the resultant). Thus `h(z)≪log(2X)` and only `O(log(2X))` possible `z` occur. This includes the rational or imaginary quadratic rank-zero cases.

Therefore every branch for which the ES identity fails as a **function-field identity** costs `O(√X)` primes unconditionally: clear denominators on its domain, obtaining a fixed proper subvariety of the surface. The same bound applies to any independently forced proper curve, regardless of the validity of the ES identity there. One cannot apply this conclusion to the *whole surface* of an identity branch.

**4. Divisor test even for genuine rational identities.** Over `ℚ(s)`, put a reduced slot in the form `t^e A(s,t)/D(s,t)` with `e∈ℤ`, `A,D∈ℤ[s,t]`, nonzero constant-in-`t` coefficients of both, and no common `t`-factor over `ℚ(s)`. If the slot has a pole away from the torus ends, then `deg_t D>0`. Compute `L(s)=Res_t(A,D)≠0` (use the usual Bézout identity for the resultant, valid also for a constant numerator). At a successful integral witness the specialized `D(√p,t)` divides `A(√p,t)` in `ℤ[√p]`, since `t` is a **unit** in that ring and the slot value an ordinary integer. It follows that `D(√p,t)|L(√p)` there. Taking quadratic norms gives

`0≠N_D := D(s,t)D(−s,−1/t)   |   L(s)L(−s)=H(P) ∈ ℤ[P]`  at `P=p`.

Ignore the finitely many zeros of `H`. As a function on `S`, `N_D` is rational and is genuinely nonconstant in `t`: its Laurent coefficient at the highest exponent is the leading `D` coefficient times the conjugate of its nonzero constant coefficient. **If `H(P)=c P^k`**, every numerical divisor is `±a p^j` for a positive divisor `a` of `|c|` and `0≤j≤k` at large primes. Thus success forces one of finitely many *proper curves* `N_D=±a P^j`, giving §3's `O(√X)` even at arbitrarily high degree. The condition is sufficient for this test, not invariant under gratuitous rescaling of `A,D`; use any valid presentation exhibiting it. More generally one may substitute any polynomial Bézout multiplier independent of `t` for the resultant.

This is nonvacuous on identity branches: set `G₁=P(x+2)/(4(x+1))`, `G₂=G₃=P(x+2)/2`. They are positive rational formulas giving `4/P` identically on the positive branch; after `t`-substitution the first has numerator `s²(t²+4t−1)` and denominator `4(t²+2t−1)`. Their resultant is a nonzero scalar times a power of `s`. Here one can see the obstruction directly: integrality requires `(x+1)|p`, so for `x>0` one has `x=p−1`; then `p|x²+1` forces `p|2`. Thus algebraic identities and positivity alone cannot be treated as success. If instead **all** slots are regular on the generic affine Pell curve (coefficients in `ℚ(P)` allowed), the two ends are exchanged by conjugation, and a nonconstant regular function has poles at both. Taking `t→∞` in an ES identity then leaves the constants already accounting for the sum; at a positive successful specialization no omitted positive term is possible. Thus, except at coefficient poles, a nonconstant all-regular identity branch never succeeds.

**Remaining gap.** General reduced denominators give `H(p)` with uncontrolled prime factors, not just factors of `cp`. A divisor count `τ(H(p))=p^o(1)` bounds choices *per prime* but does not show few primes have a choice. Siegel on each fixed fiber is not uniform in `p`; ignoring finite poles loses the divisor condition instead. I have no unconditional exclusion here for arbitrary finite menus of genuinely Pell-dependent, generically rational ES identities with nonmonomial resultants and no small-height estimate. The Hooley-based conclusion concerns successes of these fixed formulas, not the truth of ES.
