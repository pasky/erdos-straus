## Referee summary

The paper separates congruence identities polynomial in n from identities polynomial in an exponential root. It uses forced algebraic divisors to complete finite exponent coverings for three families having a genuine obstruction to finite ET/divisor coverings, supplies further coverings for ten offsets, and proves restrictions on constant-gap and constant-ratio root identities. It does not claim ES for arbitrary primes. The new claim for 15·2^s+1 removes all exponents outside 60Z, rather than removing the obstruction at exponent zero.

## Claim 1: Section 2, odd squares and Theorem 2.7 (`thm:sterile`) — SOUND

I re-derived both Jacobi-symbol arguments. In Type I, c coprime to n gives e=(a+b)/c and 4abd=ne+1; in Type II, e=4abd−n gives ce=a+b. In either case reciprocity gives (q/e)=1 for the odd part q of ab, whereas b≡−a mod e gives (ab/e)=−1. Hence ab is even, which forces e≡7 mod 8 and contradicts (2/e)=−1. All necessary coprimalities are present.

For the class corollary the fixed-parameter product really does suffice, including I1's moving c and II2/II3's moving b. The CRT construction in Theorem 2.7 chooses a square representative coprime to those parameters; primes outside the modulus are independently avoided. Divisor classes cannot contain the residue 4^J at their odd prime. Taking exponents divisible by L·ord(2) handles arbitrary L and arbitrary lower thresholds. Restriction to base 4 is legitimate. No uniformity or finite-versus-infinite-cover gap found.

The proposition classifying integer unit-square points is also sound under the positive c0,k convention of the paper. For negative exponents the numerator is odd and >1 (or has 2-adic valuation one in the even-c0 case), excluding a unit square at every odd prime.

Minor definitional issue to repair: Definition 2.4 (`def:rule`, lines 350–351) calls a finite list an exponent covering when every uncovered s is “treated separately”, without requiring that exceptional set to be finite. Taken literally this makes the non-covering claims false/vacuous. Say “all but finitely many exponents are covered, with explicit solutions for the finitely many exceptions.” This is defect D1 (MINOR); the proofs consistently use the intended finite-exception meaning.

## Claim 4: Theorem W and its non-covering corollaries — SOUND

I checked the entire reduction rather than assuming that a finite computation proves the unrestricted statement:

1. Taking coprime polynomial U,V for y/z and shrinking the exponent neighbourhood makes their numerical gcd constant (bounded by a nonzero resultant). Integer divisibility plus polynomial division then forces N/(d1d2) to be a polynomial: its proper remainder is a discrete rational number tending to zero. The crucial gcd(N,m)=1 follows separately at each odd prime dividing m from n→4^J. This removes a possible gap between numerical divisors and polynomial divisors.
2. The specialisations rho_i=d_i(1)>0 are dyadic rationals. Positivity at 1 is valid because each real factor of n(n+m) has no nonnegative root and has positive leading coefficient. The odd parts multiply to M=4^J+m. Reciprocity gives (a_i/m)=1 and hence (2/m)^(e1−e2)=−1. Therefore m≡3 mod 8 and e1−e2 is odd.
3. Since N(0) is a 2-adic unit and all d_i(0) are 2-integral, each is a unit. Every root of x has valuation 2/D, so its contribution to v2(X_i(1)/X_i(0)) is exactly −2/D. Thus e_i=−2delta_i+2J epsilon_i. This argument remains valid for arbitrarily split factors and arbitrarily large D; it is not an unjustified transfer of a small-degree factorization pattern. Integer e_i forces 2delta_i∈{0,1,2}, and odd difference forces a half-degree divisor.
4. Capelli excludes odd prime powers by v2(K)=2 and squares by sign; reducibility must be the exceptional K=4beta^4, 4|D case. Fourth-power-freeness of c0 clears beta's denominator. The magnitude bound follows from multiplying the budget congruence by the complementary odd factors; both divisibility targets are strictly positive, and B(m) decreases for m>0.
5. `scripts/review_r292_obstructions.py` derives a coarse bound at the first positive odd-w candidate, enumerates divisor triples and degree allocations independently, and filters by the exact bound. It confirms w=[1,3], [1,3,5], [3,5,7,9], [1,3,5], [1,3,5], [1,3,5] respectively and NO relation for all six excluded pairs. Positive controls give exactly m=11,19,51,131. Universal irreducibility follows from an odd-prime valuation ±1 and v2(k/(4c0))=−2; the code also checks factorization/valuation identities through D=16 as diagnostics, not as a universal proof.
6. The constant-ratio argument is sound for t∈N: the lambda_i have nonnegative 2-valuations and integer odd parts dividing t. Jacobi symbols then contradict rho1≡−rho2. Eventual periodicity and the finite-cover lemma make the passage to no finite covering legitimate.

Minor scope clarification, D2 (MINOR): the abstract/Main Theorem say “constant ratio x/n” without t∈N, whereas Definition 5.1 and Corollary 5.9 impose t∈N. This is repairable without weakening the claim: for rational t, integrality of tn near zero implies t4^J∈Z[1/2] and tk∈Z_(2), hence the denominator of t has neither an odd prime factor nor a factor 2. Positivity gives t∈N. Add this one-line reduction.

## Claim 5: moving-gap branch exclusion (Lemmas 5.13–5.14, Theorem 5.15) — SOUND

The Capelli reduction to K=15u^p is correct: an odd K cannot have K/15∈4Q^4, and a denominator v of u would satisfy v^p|15, forcing v=1. The positivity and 2-adic-root arguments really give integer X=f(1), X'=f'(1), not just dyadic rational limits. The congruences mod the odd part of ad use the explicitly stated integrality of b,c; that hypothesis must not be dropped.

I re-derived all four P≤4 bounding cases in Lemma 5.14 and independently enumerated with different bounds d≤21, m1≤36, a≤81. There are exactly 39 solutions, 31 with a,d odd, and the only relevant pair is (1,11) with K/15=3, not a p-th power for p≥3. The u≡3 mod 8 branch is excluded for all D; the even-parameter branch remains genuinely open.

The Ridout discussion is a compressed side remark rather than a proved lemma here. Its intended implication can be recovered from (X,X')≥(q−16,q−1) for q>16, yielding q≲u^(p/2) and 2^E a1=(15u^p−1)/q≳u^(p/2). The same lower bound holds immediately for q≤16. The small-a1 hypothesis then gives |u−15^(−1/p)|_2≲u^(−1−epsilon), the integer-approximation form of Ridout. No uniformity in p or effective bound is claimed. I did not access Ridout's original article; the application is secondary and is not used in any headline theorem.

## Numbered defects and concrete repairs

1. **D1 — MINOR, Definition 2.4, `paper/es-exponential-families.tex:350–351`.** Require the separately treated exceptional exponents to be *finite*. Otherwise the definition permits an empty list followed by infinitely many separate solutions and no longer implies the hypothesis used by the finite-cover lemma. Replace the final sentence by “An exponent covering is a finite list of rules covering all but finitely many s≥0, together with explicit solutions for those finitely many exceptions.”
2. **D2 — MINOR, abstract lines 60–61 / Main Theorem (d), lines 144–149, compared with Definition 5.1, lines 1062–1063 and Corollary 5.9, lines 1230–1234.** The headline says arbitrary constant ratio whereas the definition says an integer ratio. Add the rational-to-integer reduction given under Claim 4, or qualify the headline by “integer constant ratio.” This is a presentation gap, not a counterexample.
3. **D3 — MAJOR but localized, Open Problem 4, lines 1478–1482.** The parenthesis says that “48X^4+1 with X=2^t3^{t'} is handled by the rules of Theorem 4.1.” That is false without a residue restriction. Take t=0, t'=10, X=59049, n=583567942034732582449. Then X≡1 mod 11, so none of the four SG budgets applies. Meanwhile n≡4 mod 7, n≡4 mod 15 and n≡49 mod 61, so none of the six elementary rules applies either. This is reproduced by `review_r292_obstructions.py`; it is a counterexample to the asserted *rule coverage*, not to ES. The source `EXP_15.md`, Lemma 1.1, explicitly restricts the SG result to X≢0,±1 mod 11. Replace the parenthesis by: “near the boundary (a,b)=(0,1), the SG window applies because X tends to 1/2≡6 mod 11; near (3,0), cubic factorisation provides descent to a smaller Pierpont number.” Say “covered neighbourhood / descent” rather than asserting the entire 48X^4+1 two-base slice is solved.
