###### Abstract.

Let u_{n} be the n-th term of a Lucas sequence or a Lehmer sequence. In this article we shall establish an estimate from below for the greatest prime factor of u_{n} which is of the form n\exp(\log n/104\log\log n). In so doing we are able to resolve a question of Schinzel from 1962 and a conjecture of Erdős from 1965. In addition we are able to give the first general improvement on results of Bang from 1886 and Carmichael from 1912.

###### Key words and phrases:

greatest prime factor, recurrence sequences, Lucas numbers.

## 1. Introduction

Let \alpha and \beta be complex numbers such that \alpha+\beta and \alpha\beta are non-zero coprime integers and \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity. Put

u_{n}=(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})/(\alpha-\beta)\quad\text{for}\ n\geq 0.

The integers u_{n} are known as Lucas numbers and their divisibility properties have been studied by Euler, Lagrange, Gauss, Dirichlet and others (see [[11](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib11), Chapter XVII]). In 1876 Lucas [[24](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib24)] announced several new results concerning Lucas sequences (u_{n})^{\infty}_{n=0} and in a substantial paper in 1878 [[25](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib25)] he gave a systematic treatment of the divisibility properties of Lucas numbers and indicated some of the contexts in which they appeared. Much later Matijasevic [[26](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib26)] appealed to these properties in his solution of Hilbert’s 10th problem.

For any integer m let P(m) denote the greatest prime factor of m with the convention that P(m)=1 when m is 1, 0 or -1. In 1912 Carmichael [[8](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib8)] proved that if \alpha and \beta are real and n>12 then

P(u_{n})\geq n-1.(1)

Results of this character had been established earlier for integers of the form a^{n}-b^{n} where a and b are integers with a>b>0. Indeed Zsigmondy [[49](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib49)] in 1892 and Birkhoff and Vandiver [[6](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib6)] in 1904 proved that for n>2

P(a^{n}-b^{n})\geq n+1,(2)

while in the special case that b=1 the result is due to Bang [[4](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib4)] in 1886.

In 1930 Lehmer [[22](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib22)] showed that the divisibility properties of Lucas numbers hold in a more general setting. Suppose that (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are coprime non-zero integers with \alpha/\beta not a root of unity and, for n>0, put

\tilde{u}_{n}=\begin{cases}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})/(\alpha-\beta)&\text{for $n$ odd,}\\
(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n})/(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})&\text{for $n$ even.}\end{cases}

Integers of the above form have come to be known as Lehmer numbers. Observe that Lucas numbers are also Lehmer numbers up to a multiplicative factor of \alpha+\beta when n is even. In 1955 Ward [[45](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib45)] proved that if \alpha and \beta are real then for n>18,

P(\tilde{u}_{n})\geq n-1,(3)

and four years later Durst [[13](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib13)] observed that ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) holds for n>12.

A prime number p is said to be a primitive divisor of a Lucas number u_{n} if p divides u_{n} but does not divide (\alpha-\beta)^{2}u_{2}\cdots u_{n-1}. Similarly p is said to be a primitive divisor of a Lehmer number \tilde{u}_{n} if p divides \tilde{u}_{n} but does not divide (\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})^{2}\tilde{u}_{3}\cdots\tilde{u}_{n-1}. For any integer n>0 and any pair of complex numbers \alpha and \beta, we denote the n-th cyclotomic polynomial in \alpha and \beta by \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta), so

\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\prod^{n}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(j,n)=1\end{subarray}}(\alpha-\zeta^{j}\beta),

where \zeta is a primitive n-th root of unity. One may check, see [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)], that \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) is an integer for n>2 if (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are integers. Further, see Lemma 6 of [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)], if, in addition, (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are coprime non-zero integers, \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity and n>4 and n is not 6 or 12 then P(n/(3,n)) divides \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) to at most the first power and all other prime factors of \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) are congruent to 1 or -1 modulo n. The last assertion can be strengthened to all other prime factors of \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) are congruent to 1\pmod{n} in the case that \alpha and \beta are coprime integers.

Since

\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_{d}(\alpha,\beta),(4)

\Phi_{1}(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha-\beta and \Phi_{2}(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha+\beta we see that if n exceeds 2 and p is a primitive divisor of a Lucas number u_{n} or Lehmer number \tilde{u}_{n} then p divides \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta). Further, a primitive divisor of a Lucas number u_{n} or Lehmer number \tilde{u}_{n} is not a divisor of n and so it is congruent to \pm 1\pmod{n}. Estimates ([1](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([2](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) follow as consequences of the fact that the n-th term of the sequences in question possesses a primitive divisor. It was not until 1962 that this approach was extended to the case where \alpha and \beta are not real by Schinzel [[31](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib31)]. He proved, by means of an estimate for linear forms in two logarithms of algebraic numbers due to Gelfond [[17](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib17)], that there is a positive number C, which is effectively computable in terms of \alpha and \beta, such that if n exceeds C then \tilde{u}_{n} possesses a primitive divisor. In 1974 Schinzel [[35](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib35)] employed an estimate of Baker [[2](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib2)] for linear forms in the logarithms of algebraic numbers to show that C can be replaced by a positive number C_{0}, which does not depend on \alpha and \beta, and in 1977 Stewart [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib39)] showed C_{0} could be taken to be e^{452}4^{67}. This was subsequently refined by Voutier [[42](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib42), [43](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib43)] to 30030. In addition Stewart [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib39)] proved that C_{0} can be taken to be 6 for Lucas numbers and 12 for Lehmer numbers with finitely many exceptions and that the exceptions could be determined by solving a finite number of Thue equations. This program was successfully carried out by Bilu, Hanrot and Voutier [[5](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib5)] and as a consequence they were able to show that for n>30 the n-th term of a Lucas or Lehmer sequence has a primitive divisor. Thus ([1](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) hold for n>30 without the restriction that \alpha and \beta be real.

In 1962 Schinzel [[30](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib30)] asked if there exists a pair of integers a,b with ab different from \pm 2c^{2} and \pm c^{h} with h\geq 2 for which P(a^{n}-b^{n}) exceeds 2n for all sufficiently large n. In 1965 Erdős [[14](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib14)] conjectured that

\frac{P(2^{n}-1)}{n}\rightarrow\infty\quad\text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

Thirty five years later Murty and Wong [[28](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib28)] showed that Erdős’ conjecture is a consequence of the abc conjecture [[41](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib41)]. They proved, subject to the abc conjecture, that if \varepsilon is a positive real number and a and b are integers with a>b>0 then

P(a^{n}-b^{n})>n^{2-\varepsilon},

provided that n is sufficiently large in terms of a,b and \varepsilon. In 2004 Murata and Pomerance [[27](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib27)] proved, subject to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, that

P(2^{n}-1)>n^{4/3}/\log\log n(5)

for a set of positive integers n of asymptotic density 1.

The first unconditional refinement of ([2](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) was obtained by Schinzel [[30](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib30)] in 1962. He proved that if a and b are coprime and ab is a square or twice a square then

P(a^{n}-b^{n})\geq 2n+1

provided that one excludes the cases n=4,6,12 when a=2 and b=1. Schinzel proved his result by showing that the term a^{n}-b^{n} was divisible by at least 2 primitive divisors. To prove this result he appealed to an Aurifeuillian factorization of \Phi_{n}. Rotkiewicz [[29](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib29)] extended Schinzel’s argument to treat Lucas numbers and then Schinzel [[32](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib32), [33](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib33), [34](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib34)] in a sequence of articles gave conditions under which Lehmer numbers possess at least 2 primitive divisors and so under which ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) holds with n+1 in place of n-1, see also [[21](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib21)]. In 1975 Stewart [[37](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib37)] proved that if \kappa is a positive real number with \kappa<1/\log 2 then P(a^{n}-b^{n})/n tends to infinity with n provided that n runs through those integers with at most \kappa\log\log n distinct prime factors, see also [[15](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib15)]. Stewart [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)] in the case that \alpha and \beta are real and Shorey and Stewart [[36](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib36)] in the case that \alpha and \beta are not real generalized this work to Lucas and Lehmer sequences. Let \alpha and \beta be complex numbers such that (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are non-zero relatively prime integers with \alpha/\beta not a root of unity. For any positive integer n let \omega(n) denote the number of distinct prime factors of n and put q(n)=2^{\omega(n)}, the number of square-free divisors of n. Further let \varphi(n) be the number of positive integers less than or equal to n and coprime with n. They showed, recall ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), if n\ (>3) has at most \kappa\log\log n distinct prime factors then

P(\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta))>C(\varphi(n)\log n)/q(n),(6)

where C is a positive number which is effectively computable in terms of \alpha,\beta and \kappa only. The proofs depend on lower bounds for linear forms in the logarithms of algebraic numbers in the complex case when \alpha and \beta are real and in the p-adic case otherwise.

The purpose of the present paper is to answer in the affirmative the question posed by Schinzel [[30](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib30)] and to prove Erdős’ conjecture in the wider context of Lucas and Lehmer numbers.

###### Theorem 1.

Let \alpha and \beta be complex numbers such that (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are non-zero integers and \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity. There exists a positive number C, which is effectively computable in terms of \omega(\alpha\beta) and the discriminant of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta), such that for n>C,

P(\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta))>n\exp(\log n/104\log\log n).(7)

Our result, with the aid of ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) gives an improvement of ([1](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([2](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E2 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E3 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([6](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), answers the question of Schinzel and proves the conjecture of Erdős. Specifically, if a and b are integers with a>b>0 then

P(a^{n}-b^{n})>n\exp(\log n/104\log\log n),(8)

for n sufficiently large in terms of the number of distinct prime factors of ab. We remark that the factor 104 which occurs on the right hand side of ([7](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E7 "In Theorem 1. ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) has no arithmetical significance. Instead it is determined by the current quality of the estimates for linear forms in p-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers. In fact we could replace 104 by any number strictly larger than 14e^{2}. The proof depends upon estimates for linear forms in the logarithms of algebraic numbers in the complex and the p-adic case. In particular it depends upon [[48](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib48)] where improvements upon the dependence on the parameter p in the lower bounds for linear forms in p-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers are established. This allows us to estimate directly the order of primes dividing \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta). The estimates are non-trivial for small primes and, coupled with an estimate from below for |\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|, they allow us to show that we must have a large prime divisor of \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) since otherwise the total non-archimedean contribution from the primes does not balance that of |\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|. By contrast for the proof of ([6](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E6 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) a much weaker assumption on the greatest prime factor is imposed and it leads to the conclusion that then \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) is divisible by many small primes. This part of the argument from [[36](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib36)] and [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)] was also employed in Murata and Pomerance’s [[27](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib27)] proof of ([5](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E5 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and in estimates of Stewart [[40](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib40)] for the greatest square-free factor of \tilde{u}_{n}.

For any non-zero integer x let ord{}_{p}x denote the p-adic order of x. Our next result follows from a special case of Lemma 8 of this paper. Lemma 8 yields a crucial step in the proof of Theorem 1. An unusual feature of the proof of Lemma 8 is that we artificially inflate the number of terms which occur in the p-adic linear form in logarithms which appears in the argument. We have chosen to highlight it in the integer case.

###### Theorem 2.

Let a and b be integers with a>b>0. There exists a number C_{1}, which is effectively computable in terms of \omega(ab), such that if p is a prime number which does not divide ab and which exceeds C_{1} and n is an integer with n\geq 2 then

\text{\rm ord}_{p}(a^{n}-b^{n})<p\exp(-\log p/52\log\log p)\log a+\text{\rm ord}_{p}n.(9)

If a and b are integers with a>b>0, p is an odd prime number which does not divide ab and n\geqslant 2 then, as in the proof of Theorem 2,

\text{\rm ord}_{p}(a^{n}-b^{n})\leq\text{\rm ord}_{p}(a^{p-1}-b^{p-1})+\text{\rm ord}_{p}n.

In particular if p exceeds C_{1} then

\text{\rm ord}_{p}(a^{p-1}-b^{p-1})<~p\exp(-\log p/52\log\log p)\log a.

Yamada [[46](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib46)], by making use of a refinement of an estimate of Bugeaud and Laurent [[7](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib7)] for linear forms in two p-adic logarithms, proved that there is a positive number C_{2}, which is effectively computable in terms of \omega(a), such that

\text{\rm ord}_{p}(a^{p-1}-1)<C_{2}(p/(\log p)^{2})\log a.(10)

By following our proof of Theorem 1 and using (10) in place of Lemma 8 it is possible to show that there exist positive numbers C_{3},C_{4} and C_{5}, which are effectively computable in terms of \omega(a) such that if n exceeds C_{3} then

P(a^{n}-1)>C_{4}~\varphi(n)(\log n\log\log n)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and so, by Theorem 328 of [[19](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib19)],

P(a^{n}-1)>C_{5}~n(\log n/\log\log n)^{\frac{1}{2}}.(11)

This gives an alternative proof of the conjecture of Erdős, although the lower bound (11) is weaker than the bound (8).

## 2. Preliminary lemmas

Let \alpha and \beta be complex numbers such that (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are non-zero integers and \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity. We shall assume, without loss of generality, that

|\alpha|\geq|\beta|.

Observe that

\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{r}+\sqrt{s}}{2},\quad\beta=\frac{\sqrt{r}-\sqrt{s}}{2}

where r and s are non-zero integers with |r|\neq|s|. Further \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{rs}). Note that (\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})^{2}=rs and we may write rs in the form m^{2}d with m a positive integer and d a square-free integer so that \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{rs})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}).

For any algebraic number \gamma let h(\gamma) denote the absolute logarithmic height of \gamma. In particular if a_{0}(x-\gamma_{1})\cdots(x-\gamma_{d}) in \mathbb{Z}[x] is the minimal polynomial of \gamma over \mathbb{Z} then

h(\gamma)=\frac{1}{d}\left(\log a_{0}+\sum^{d}_{j=1}\log\max(1,|\gamma_{j}|)\right).

Notice that

\alpha\beta(x-\alpha/\beta)(x-\beta/\alpha)=\alpha\beta x^{2}-(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})x+\alpha\beta=\alpha\beta x^{2}-((\alpha+\beta)^{2}-2\alpha\beta)x+\alpha\beta

is a polynomial with integer coefficients and so either \alpha/\beta is rational or the polynomial is a multiple of the minimal polynomial of \alpha/\beta. Therefore we have

h(\alpha/\beta)\leq\log|\alpha|.(12)

We first record a result describing the prime factors of \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta).

###### Lemma 1.

Suppose that (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are coprime. If n>4 and n\neq 6,12 then P(n/(3n)) divides \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) to at most the first power. All other prime factors of \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) are congruent to \pm 1\pmod{n}.

###### Proof.

This is Lemma 6 of [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)]. ∎

Let K be a finite extension of \mathbb{Q} and let \wp be a prime ideal in the ring of algebraic integers \mathcal{O}_{K} of K. Let \mathcal{O}_{\wp} consist of 0 and the non-zero elements \alpha of K for which \wp has a non-negative exponent in the canonical decomposition of the fractional ideal generated by \alpha into prime ideals. Then let P be the unique prime ideal of \mathcal{O}_{\wp} and put \overline{K_{\wp}}=\mathcal{O}_{\wp}/P. Further for any \alpha in \mathcal{O}_{\wp} we let \overline{\alpha} be the image of \alpha under the residue class map that sends \alpha to \alpha+P in \overline{K_{\wp}}.

Our next result is motivated by work of Lucas [[25](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib25)] and Lehmer [[22](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib22)].

###### Lemma 2.

Let d be a square-free integer different from 1,\theta be an algebraic integer of degree 2 over \mathbb{Q} in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}) and let \theta^{\prime} denote the algebraic conjugate of \theta over \mathbb{Q}. Suppose that p is a prime which does not divide 2\theta\theta^{\prime}. Let \wp be a prime ideal of the ring of algebraic integers of \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}) lying above p. The order of \overline{\theta/\theta^{\prime}} in (\overline{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}})_{\wp})^{\times} is a divisor of 2 if p divides (\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2})^{2} and a divisor of p-(d/p) otherwise.

###### Proof.

We first note that \theta and \theta^{\prime} are p-adic units. If p divides (\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2})^{2} then either p divides (\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{2} or p divides \theta+\theta^{\prime} and in both cases (\theta/\theta^{\prime})^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{\wp}. Thus the order of \overline{\theta/\theta^{\prime}} divides 2.

Thus we may suppose that p does not divide 2\theta\theta^{\prime}(\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2})^{2} and, in particular, p\nmid d. Since

2\theta=(\theta+\theta^{\prime})+(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad 2\theta^{\prime}=(\theta+\theta^{\prime})-(\theta-\theta^{\prime})(13)

we see, on raising both sides of the above equations to the p-th power and subtracting, that 2^{p}(\theta^{p}-\theta^{\prime p})-2(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{p} is p(\theta-\theta^{\prime}) times an algebraic integer. Hence, since p is odd,

\frac{\theta^{p}-\theta^{\prime p}}{\theta-\theta^{\prime}}\equiv(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{p-1}\pmod{p}.

But

(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{p-1}=((\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{2})^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\equiv\left(\frac{(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{2}}{p}\right)\pmod{p}

and

\left(\frac{(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{2}}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{d}{p}\right),

so

\frac{\theta^{p}-\theta^{\prime p}}{\theta-\theta^{\prime}}\equiv\left(\frac{d}{p}\right)\pmod{p}.(14)

By raising both sides of equation ([13](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E13 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) to the p-th power and adding we find that

\frac{\theta^{p}+\theta^{\prime p}}{\theta+\theta^{\prime}}\equiv(\theta+\theta^{\prime})^{p-1}\pmod{p}

and since \left(\frac{(\theta+\theta^{\prime})^{2}}{p}\right)=1,

\frac{\theta^{p}+\theta^{\prime p}}{\theta+\theta^{\prime}}\equiv 1\pmod{p}.(15)

If \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=-1 then adding ([14](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E14 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([15](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E15 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) we find that

2\frac{\theta^{p+1}-\theta^{\prime p+1}}{\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2}}\equiv 0\pmod{p}

hence, since p does not divide 2\theta\theta^{\prime}(\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2})^{2},

(\theta/\theta^{\prime})^{p+1}\equiv 1\pmod{\wp}

and the result follows. If \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=1 then subtracting ([14](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E14 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([15](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E15 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) we find that

2\theta\theta^{\prime}\frac{\theta^{p-1}-\theta^{\prime p-1}}{\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2}}\equiv 0\pmod{p}

hence, since p does not divide 2\theta\theta^{\prime}(\theta^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2})^{2},

\left(\theta/\theta^{\prime}\right)^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{\wp}

and this completes the proof. ∎

Let \ell and n be integers with n\geq 1 and for each real number x let \pi(x,n,\ell) denote the number of primes not greater than x and congruent to \ell modulo n. We require a version of the Brun-Titchmarsh theorem, see [[18](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib18), Theorem 3.8].

###### Lemma 3.

If 1\leq n<x and (n,\ell)=1 then

\pi(x,n,\ell)<3x/(\varphi(n)\log(x/n)).

Our next result gives an estimate for the primes p below a given bound which occur as the norm of an algebraic integer in the ring of algebraic integers of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta).

###### Lemma 4.

Let d be a squarefree integer with d\neq 1 and let p_{k} denote the k-th smallest prime of the form N\pi_{k}=p_{k} where N denotes the norm from \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}) to \mathbb{Q} and \pi_{k} is an algebraic integer in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}). Let \varepsilon be a positive real number. There is a positive number C, which is effectively computable in terms of \varepsilon and d, such that if k exceeds C then

\log p_{k}<(1+\varepsilon)\log k.

###### Proof.

Let K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}) and denote the ring of algebraic integers of K by \mathcal{O}_{K}. A prime p is the norm of an element \pi of \mathcal{O}_{K} provided that it is representable as the value of the primitive quadratic form q_{K}(x,y) given by x^{2}-dy^{2} if d\not\equiv 1\pmod{4} and x^{2}+xy+\left(\frac{1-d}{4}\right)y^{2} if d\equiv 1\pmod{4}. By [[16](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib16), Chapter VII, (2.14)], a prime p is represented by q_{K}(x,y) if and only if p is not inert in K and all prime ideals \wp of \mathcal{O}_{K} above p have trivial narrow class in the narrow ideal class group of K. Let K_{H} be the strict Hilbert class field of K.K_{H} is normal over K and G, the Galois group of K_{H} over K, is isomorphic with the narrow ideal class group of K and so |G|=h^{+}, the strict ideal class number of K, see Theorem 7.1.2 of [[10](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib10)]. The prime ideals \wp of \mathcal{O}_{K} which do not ramify in K_{H} and which are principal are the only prime ideals of \mathcal{O}_{K} which do not ramify in K_{H} and which split completely in K_{H}, see Theorem 7.1.3 of [[10](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib10)]. These prime ideals may be counted by the Chebotarev Density Theorem. Let \left[\frac{K_{H}/K}{\wp}\right] denote the conjugacy class of Frobenius automorphisms corresponding to prime ideals P of \mathcal{O}_{K_{H}} above \wp. In particular, for each conjugacy class C of G we define \pi_{C}(x,K_{H}/K) to be the cardinality of the set of prime ideals \wp of \mathcal{O}_{K} which are unramified in K_{H}, for which \left[\frac{K_{H}/K}{\wp}\right]=C and for which N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\wp\leq x. Denote by C_{0} the conjugacy class consisting of the identity element of G. Note that the number of inert primes p of \mathcal{O}_{K} for which N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\ p\leq x is at most x^{1/2}. Thus the number of primes p up to x for which p is the norm of an element \pi of \mathcal{O}_{K} is bounded from below by

\pi_{C_{0}}(x,K_{H}/K)-x^{1/2}.(16)

It follows from Theorem 1.3 and 1.4 of [[23](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib23)] that there is a positive number C_{1}, which is effectively computable in terms of d, such that for x greater than C_{1} the quantity ([16](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E16 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) exceeds

\frac{x}{2h^{+}\log x}.

Further

\frac{x}{2h^{+}\log x}>k

when x is at least 4h^{+}k\log k and

k/\log k>4h^{+}.(17)

Thus, provided ([17](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E17 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) holds and x exceeds C_{1},

p_{k}<4h^{+}k\log k.(18)

Our result now follows from ([18](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E18 "In Proof. ‣ 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) on taking logarithms. ∎

## 3. Estimates for linear forms in p-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers

Let \alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n} be non-zero algebraic numbers and put K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}) and d=[K:\mathbb{Q}]. Let \wp be a prime ideal of the ring \mathcal{O}_{K} of algebraic integers in K lying above the prime number p. Denote by e_{\wp} the ramification index of \wp and by f_{\wp} the residue class degree of \wp. For \alpha in K with \alpha\neq 0 let ord{}_{\wp}\alpha be the exponent to which \wp divides the principal fractional ideal generated by \alpha in K and put ord{}_{\wp}0=\infty. For any positive integer m let \zeta_{m}=e^{2\pi i/m} and put \alpha_{0}=\zeta_{2^{u}} where \zeta_{2^{u}}\in K and \zeta_{2^{u+1}}\not\in K.

Suppose that \alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n} are multiplicatively independent \wp-adic units in K. Let \overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\overline{\alpha_{n}} be the images of \alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n} under the residue class map at \wp from the ring of \wp-adic integers in K onto the residue class field \overline{K}_{\wp} at \wp. For any set X let |X| denote its cardinality. Let \langle\overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\overline{\alpha_{n}}\rangle be the subgroup of (\overline{K}_{\wp})^{\times} generated by \overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\overline{\alpha_{n}}. We define \delta by

\delta=1\quad\text{ if }\quad[K(\alpha_{0}^{1/2},\alpha_{1}^{1/2},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{1/2}):K]<2^{n+1}

and

\delta=(p^{f_{\wp}}-1)/|\langle\overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\overline{\alpha_{n}}\rangle|

if

[K(\alpha_{0}^{1/2},\alpha_{1}^{1/2},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{1/2}):K]=2^{n+1}.(19)

###### Lemma 5.

Let p be a prime with p\geq 5 and let \wp be an unramified prime ideal of \mathcal{O}_{K} lying above p. Let \alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n} be multiplicatively independent \wp-adic units. Let b_{1},\dots,b_{n} be integers, not all zero, and put

B=\max(2,|b_{1}|,\dots,|b_{n}|).

Then

\text{\rm ord}_{\wp}(\alpha_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}-1)<Ch(\alpha_{1})\cdots h(\alpha_{n})\max(\log B,(n+1)(5.4n+\log d))

where

\begin{split}C&=376(n+1)^{1/2}\left(7e\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)^{n}d^{n+2}\log^{*}d\log(e^{4}(n+1)d)\cdot\\
&\qquad\max\left(\frac{p^{f_{p}}}{\delta}\left(\frac{n}{f_{p}\log p}\right)^{n},e^{n}f_{p}\log p\right).\end{split}

###### Proof.

We apply the Main Theorem of [[48](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib48)] and in (1.16) we take C_{1}(n,d,\wp,a)h^{(1)} in place of the minimum. Further (1.15) holds since our result is symmetric in the b_{i}’s. Next we note that, since \wp is unramified and p\geq 5, we may take c^{(1)}=1794,a^{(1)}=7\frac{p-1}{p-2},a^{(1)}_{0}=2+\log 7 and a^{(1)}_{1}=a^{(1)}_{2}=5.25. We remark that condition ([19](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S3.E19 "In 3. Estimates for linear forms in 𝑝-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) ensures that we may take \{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}\} to be \{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}. Finally the explicit version of Dobrowolski’s Theorem due to Voutier [[44](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib44)] allows us to replace the first term in the maximum defining h^{(1)} by \log B. Therefore we find that

\text{ord}_{\wp}(\alpha_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}-1)<C_{1}h(\alpha_{1})\cdots h(\alpha_{n})\max(\log B,G_{1},(n+1)f_{\wp}\log p)

where

G_{1}=(n+1)((2+\log 7)n+5.25+\log((2+\log 7)n+5.25)+\log d)

and, on denoting \log\max(x,e) by \log^{*}x,

\begin{split}C_{1}&=1794\left(7\left(\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)\right)^{n}\frac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{n!}\,\frac{d^{n+2}\log^{*}d}{2^{u}(f_{\wp}\log p)^{2}}\\
&\qquad\cdot\max\left(\frac{p^{f_{\wp}}}{\delta}\left(\frac{n}{f_{\wp}\log p}\right)^{n},e^{n}f_{\wp}\log p\right)\cdot\max(\log(e^{4}(n+1)d),f_{\wp}\log p).\end{split}

Note that 2^{u}\geq 2 and f_{\wp}\log p\geq\log 5. Further, by Stirling’s formula, see 6.1.38 of [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib1)],

\frac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{n!}\leq\frac{e^{n+1}(n+1)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

and so

\text{ord}_{\wp}(\alpha_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}-1)<C_{2}h(\alpha_{1})\cdots h(\alpha_{n})\max\left(\frac{\log B}{\log 5},\frac{G_{1}}{\log 5},n+1\right)(20)

where

\begin{split}C_{2}&=\frac{1794}{2}\,\frac{e}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(n+1)^{1/2}\left(7e\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)^{n}d^{n+2}\log^{*}d\\
&\qquad\max\left(\frac{p^{f_{\wp}}}{\delta}\left(\frac{n}{f_{\wp}\log p}\right)^{n},e^{n}f_{\wp}\log p\right)\frac{(\log(e^{4}(n+1)d)}{\log 5}.\end{split}(21)

We next observe that

G_{1}\leq(n+1)(5.4n+\log d)

and as a consequence

\max\left(\frac{\log B}{\log 5},\frac{G_{1}}{\log 5},n+1\right)=\max\left(\frac{\log B}{\log 5},\frac{(n+1)(5.4n+\log d)}{\log 5}\right).(22)

The result now follows from ([20](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S3.E20 "In Proof. ‣ 3. Estimates for linear forms in 𝑝-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([21](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S3.E21 "In Proof. ‣ 3. Estimates for linear forms in 𝑝-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([22](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S3.E22 "In Proof. ‣ 3. Estimates for linear forms in 𝑝-adic logarithms of algebraic numbers ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")). ∎

## 4. Further preliminaries

Let (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta be non-zero integers with \alpha/\beta not a root of unity. We may suppose that |\alpha|\geq|\beta|. Since there is a positive number c_{0} which exceeds 1 such that |\alpha|\geq c_{0} we deduce from Lemma 3 of [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib39)], see also Lemmas 1 and 2 of [[35](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib35)], that there is a positive number c_{1} which we may suppose exceeds (\log c_{0})^{-1} such that for n>0

\log 2+n\log|\alpha|\geq\log|\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}|\geq(n-c_{1}\log(n+1))\log|\alpha|.(23)

The proof of ([23](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E23 "In 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) depends upon an estimate for a linear form in the logarithms of two algebraic numbers due to Baker [[2](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib2)].

For any positive integer n let \mu(n) denote the Möbius function of n. It follows from ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) that

\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{d\mid n}(\alpha^{n/d}-\beta^{n/d})^{\mu(d)}.(24)

We may now deduce, following the approach of [[35](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib35)] and [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib39)], our next result.

###### Lemma 6.

There exists an effectively computable positive number c such that if n>2 then

|\alpha|^{\varphi(n)-cq(n)\log n}\leq|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|\leq|\alpha|^{\varphi(n)+cq(n)\log n},(25)

where q(n)=2^{\omega(n)}.

###### Proof.

By ([24](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E24 "In 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers"))

\log|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\log|\alpha^{n/d}-\beta^{n/d}|

and so by ([23](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E23 "In 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers"))

\left|\log|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|-\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\frac{n}{d}\log|\alpha|\right|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
\mu(d)\neq 0\end{subarray}}c_{1}\log(n+1)\log|\alpha|

since c_{1} exceeds (\log c_{0})^{-1}. Our result now follows. ∎

###### Lemma 7.

There exists an effectively computable positive number c_{1} such that if n exceeds c_{1} then

\log|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|\geq\frac{\varphi(n)}{2}\log|\alpha|.(26)

###### Proof.

For n sufficiently large

\varphi(n)>n/2\log\log n\quad\text{and}\quad q(n)<n^{1/\log\log n}.

Since |\alpha|\geq c_{0}>1 it follows from ([25](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E25 "In Lemma 6. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) that if n is sufficiently large

|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|>|\alpha|^{\varphi(n)/2},

as required. ∎

###### Lemma 8.

Let n be an integer larger than 1, let p be a prime which does not divide \alpha\beta and let \wp be a prime ideal of the ring of algebraic integers of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) lying above p which does not ramify. Then there exists a positive number C, which is effectively computable in terms of \omega(\alpha\beta) and the discriminant of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta), such that if p exceeds C then

\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)<p\exp(-\log p/51.9\log\log p)\log|\alpha|\log n.

###### Proof.

Let c_{1},c_{2},\dots denote positive numbers which are effectively computable in terms of \omega(\alpha\beta) and the discriminant of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta). We remark that since \alpha/\beta is of degree at most 2 over \mathbb{Q} the discriminant of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) determines the field \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) and so knowing it one may compute the class number and regulator of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) as well as the strict Hilbert class field of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) and the discriminant of this field. Further let p be a prime which does not divide 6d\alpha\beta where d is defined as in the first paragraph of §2.

Put K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) and

\alpha_{0}=\begin{cases}i&\text{if}\ i\in K\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let v be the largest integer for which

\alpha/\beta=\alpha^{j}_{0}\theta^{2^{v}},(27)

with 0\leq j\leq 3 and \theta in K. To see that there is a largest such integer we first note that either there is a prime ideal \mathfrak{q} of \mathcal{O}_{K}, the ring of algebraic integers of K, lying above a prime q which occurs to a positive exponent in the principal fractional ideal generated by \alpha/\beta or \alpha/\beta is a unit. In the former case h(\alpha/\beta)\geq 2^{v-1}\log q and in the latter case, since \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity, there is a positive number c_{1}, see [[12](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib12)], such that h(\alpha/\beta)\geq 2^{v}c_{1}.

Notice from ([27](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E27 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) that

h(\alpha/\beta)=2^{v}h(\theta).(28)

Further, by Kummer theory, see Lemma 3 of [[3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib3)],

[K(\alpha^{1/2}_{0},\theta^{1/2}):K]=4.(29)

Furthermore since p\nmid\alpha\beta and \alpha and \beta are algebraic integers

\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)\leq\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{4n}-1).(30)

For any real number x let [x] denote the greatest integer less than or equal to x. Put

k=\left[\frac{\log p}{51.8\log\log p}\right].(31)

Then, for p>c_{2}, we find that k\geq 2 and

\max\left(p\left(\frac{k}{\log p}\right)^{k},e^{k}\log p\right)=p\left(\frac{k}{\log p}\right)^{k}.(32)

Our proof splits into two cases. We shall first suppose that \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta)=\mathbb{Q} so that \alpha and \beta are integers. For any positive integer j with j\geq 2 let p_{j} denote the j-1-th smallest prime which does not divide p\alpha\beta. We put

m=n2^{v+2}(33)

and

\alpha_{1}=\theta/p_{2}\cdots p_{k}.

Then

\theta^{m}-1=\left(\frac{\theta}{p_{2}\cdots p_{k}}\right)^{m}p^{m}_{2}\cdots p^{m}_{k}-1=\alpha^{m}_{1}p^{m}_{2}\cdots p^{m}_{k}-1(34)

and by ([27](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E27 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([30](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E30 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([33](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E33 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([34](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E34 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers"))

\text{ord}_{p}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)\leq\text{ord}_{p}(\alpha^{m}_{1}p^{m}_{2}\cdots p^{m}_{k}-1).(35)

Note that \alpha_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k} are multiplicatively independent since \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity and p_{2},\dots,p_{k} are primes which do not divide p\alpha\beta. Further, since p_{2},\dots,p_{k} are different from p and p does not divide \alpha\beta, we see that \alpha_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k} are p-adic units.

We now apply Lemma 5 with \delta=1, d=1,f_{\wp}=1 and n=k to conclude that

\begin{split}\text{ord}_{p}(\alpha^{m}_{1}p^{m}_{2}\cdots p^{m}_{k}-1)&\leq c_{3}(k+1)^{3}\log p\left(7e\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)^{k}\\
&\qquad\max\left(p\left(\frac{k}{\log p}\right)^{k},e^{k}\right)(\log m)h(\alpha_{1})\log p_{2}\cdots\log p_{k}.\end{split}(36)

Put

t=\omega(\alpha\beta).

Let q_{i} denote the i-th prime number. Note that

p_{k}\leq q_{k+t+1}

and thus

\log p_{2}+\cdots+\log p_{k}\leq(k-1)\log q_{k+t+1}.

By the prime number theorem with error term, for k>c_{4},

\log p_{2}+\cdots+\log p_{k}\leq 1.001(k-1)\log k.(37)

By the arithmetic geometric mean inequality

\log p_{2}\cdots\log p_{k}\leq\left(\frac{\log p_{2}+\cdots+\log p_{k}}{k-1}\right)^{k-1}

and so, by ([37](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E37 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

\log p_{2}\cdots\log p_{k}\leq(1.001\log k)^{k-1}.(38)

Since h(\alpha_{1})\leq h(\theta)+\log p_{2}\cdots p_{k} it follows from ([37](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E37 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) that

h(\alpha_{1})\leq c_{5}h(\theta)k\log k.(39)

Further m=2^{v+2}n is at most n^{2^{v+2}} and so by ([12](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E12 "In 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([28](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E28 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers"))

h(\theta)\log m\leq 4h(\alpha/\beta)\log n\leq 4\log|\alpha|\log n.(40)

Thus, by ([32](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E32 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([35](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E35 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([36](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E36 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E38 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([39](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E39 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([40](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E40 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

\text{ord}_{p}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)<c_{6}k^{4}\log p\left(7e\frac{p-1}{p-2}1.001\frac{k\log k}{\log p}\right)^{k}p\log|\alpha|\log n.

Therefore, by ([31](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E31 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), for p>c_{7}

\text{ord}_{p}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)<pe^{-\frac{\log p}{51.9\log\log p}}\log|\alpha|\log n.(41)

We now suppose that [\mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta):\mathbb{Q}]=2. Let \pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{k} be elements of \mathcal{O}_{K} with the property that N(\pi_{i})=p_{i} where N denotes the norm from K to \mathbb{Q} and where p_{i} is a rational prime number which does not divide 2dp\alpha\beta. We now put \theta_{i}=\pi_{i}/\pi^{\prime}_{i} where \pi^{\prime}_{i} denotes the algebraic conjugate of \pi_{i} in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta). Notice that p does not divide \pi_{i}\pi^{\prime}_{i}=p_{i} and if p does not divide (\pi_{i}-\pi^{\prime}_{i})^{2} then

\left(\frac{(\pi_{i}-\pi^{\prime}_{i})^{2}}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{d}{p}\right),

since \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})=\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{i}). Thus, by Lemma 2, the order of \theta_{i} in (\overline{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta})_{\wp})^{\times} is a divisor of 2 if p divides \pi^{2}_{i}-\pi^{\prime 2}_{i} and a divisor of p-\left(\frac{d}{p}\right) otherwise. Since p is odd and p does not divide d we conclude that the order of \theta_{i} in (\overline{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta})_{\wp})^{\times} is a divisor of p-\left(\frac{d}{p}\right).

Put

\alpha_{1}=\theta/\theta_{2}\cdots\theta_{k}.(42)

Then

\theta^{m}-1=\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{2}\cdots\theta_{k}}\right)^{m}\theta^{m}_{2}\cdots\theta^{m}_{k}-1

and, by ([27](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E27 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([30](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E30 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([33](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E33 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([42](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E42 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)\leq\text{ord}_{\wp}(\alpha^{m}_{1}\theta^{m}_{2}\cdots\theta^{m}_{k}-1).(43)

Observe that \alpha_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k} are multiplicatively independent since \alpha/\beta is not a root of unity, p_{2},\dots,p_{k} are primes which do not divide \alpha\beta and the principal prime ideals [\pi_{i}] for i=2,\dots,k do not ramify since p\nmid 2d. Further since p_{2},\dots,p_{k} are different from p and p does not divide \alpha\beta we see that \alpha_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k} are \wp-adic units.

Notice that

K(\alpha^{1/2}_{0},\theta^{1/2},\theta^{1/2}_{2},\dots,\theta^{1/2}_{k})=K(\alpha^{1/2}_{0},\alpha^{1/2}_{1},\theta^{1/2}_{2},\dots,\theta^{1/2}_{k}).

Further

[K(\alpha^{1/2}_{0},\theta^{1/2},\theta^{1/2}_{2},\dots,\theta^{1/2}_{k}):K]=2^{k+1},(44)

since otherwise, by ([29](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E29 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and Kummer theory, see Lemma 3 of [[3](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib3)], there is an integer i with 2\leq i\leq k and integers j_{0},\dots,j_{i-1} with 0\leq j_{b}\leq 1 for b=0,\dots,i-1 and an element \gamma of K for which

\theta_{i}=\alpha^{j_{0}}_{0}\theta^{j_{1}}\theta^{j_{2}}_{2}\cdots\theta^{j_{i-1}}_{i-1}\gamma^{2}.(45)

But the order of the prime ideal [\pi_{i}] on the left-hand side of ([45](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E45 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) is 1 whereas the order on the right-hand side of ([45](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E45 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) is even which is a contradiction. Thus ([44](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E44 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) holds.

Since p does not divide the discriminant of K and [K:\mathbb{Q}]=2 either p splits, in which case f_{\wp}=1 and \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=1, or p is inert, in which case f_{\wp}=2 and \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=-1, see [[20](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib20)]. Observe that if \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=1 then

p^{f_{p}}/\delta\leq p.(46)

Let us now determine |\langle\overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\theta},\overline{\theta_{2}},\dots,\overline{\theta_{k}}\rangle| in the case \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=-1. By our earlier remarks the order of \overline{\theta_{i}} is a divisor of p+1 for i=2,\dots,k. Further by ([27](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E27 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) since N(\alpha/\beta)=1 we find that N(\theta)=\pm 1 and so N(\theta^{2})=1. By Hilbert’s Theorem 90, see eg. Theorem 14.35 of [[9](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib9)], \theta^{2}=\varrho/\varrho^{\prime} where \varrho and \varrho^{\prime} are conjugate algebraic integers in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta). Note that we may suppose that the principal ideals [\varrho] and [\varrho^{\prime}] have no principal ideal divisors in common. Further since p does not divide \alpha\beta and, since \left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=-1,[p] is a principal prime ideal of \mathcal{O}_{K} and we note that p does not divide \varrho\varrho^{\prime}. It follows from Lemma 2 that the order of \theta^{2} in (\overline{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta})_{\wp})^{\times} is a divisor of p+1 hence \theta has order a divisor of 2(p+1). Since \alpha^{4}_{0}=1 we conclude that

|\langle\overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\theta},\overline{\theta_{2}},\dots,\overline{\alpha_{k}}\rangle|\leq 2(p+1)

and so

\delta=(p^{2}-1)/|\langle\overline{\alpha_{0}},\overline{\theta},\overline{\theta_{2}},\dots,\overline{\theta_{k}}\rangle|\geq(p-1)/2.(47)

We now apply Lemma 5 noting, by ([46](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E46 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([47](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E47 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), that

p^{f_{p}}/\delta\leq 2p^{2}/(p-1).

Thus, by ([32](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E32 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

\begin{split}\text{ord}_{\wp}(\alpha^{m}_{1}\theta^{m}_{2}\cdots\theta^{m}_{k}-1)&\leq c_{8}(k+1)^{3}\log p\left(7e\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)^{k}2^{k}p\left(\frac{k}{\log p}\right)^{k}\\
&\qquad(\log m)h(\alpha_{1})h(\theta_{2})\cdots h(\theta_{k}).\end{split}(48)

Notice that \theta_{i}=\pi_{i}/\pi^{\prime}_{i} and that p_{i}(x-\pi_{i}/\pi^{\prime}_{i})(x-\pi^{\prime}_{i}/\pi_{i})=p_{i}x^{2}-(\pi^{2}_{i}+\pi^{\prime 2}_{i})x+p_{i} is the minimal polynomial of \theta_{i} over the integers since [\pi_{i}] is unramified. Either the discriminant of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta) is negative in which case |\pi_{i}|=|\pi^{\prime}_{i}| or it is positive in which case there is a fundamental unit \varepsilon>1 in \mathcal{O}_{K}. We may replace \pi_{i} by \pi_{i}\varepsilon^{u} for any integer u and so without loss of generality we may suppose that p^{1/2}_{i}\leq|\pi_{i}|\leq p^{1/2}_{i}\varepsilon and consequently that p_{i}^{1/2}\varepsilon^{-1}\leq|\pi^{\prime}_{i}|\leq p_{i}^{1/2}. Therefore

h(\theta_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log p_{i}\varepsilon^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\log p_{i}+\log\varepsilon\quad\text{for}\ d>0

and

h(\theta_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log p_{i}\quad\text{for}\ d<0.

Let us put

R=\begin{cases}\log\varepsilon&\text{for}\ d>0\\
0&\text{for}\ d<0.\end{cases}

Then

h(\theta_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log p_{i}+R(49)

for i=2,\dots,k. In a similar fashion we find that

h(\theta_{2}\cdots\theta_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log p_{2}\cdots p_{k}+R,(50)

and so

h(\alpha_{1})\leq h(\theta)+\frac{1}{2}\log p_{2}\cdots p_{k}+R.(51)

Put

t_{1}=\omega(2dp\alpha\beta).

Let q_{i} denote the i-th prime number which is representable as the norm of an element of \mathcal{O}_{K}. Note that

p_{k}\leq q_{k+t_{1}}

and thus

\log p_{2}+\cdots+\log p_{k}\leq(k-1)\log q_{k+t_{1}}.

Therefore by Lemma 4 for k>c_{9},

\log p_{2}+\cdots+\log p_{k}\leq 1.0005(k-1)\log k(52)

and so, as for the proof of ([38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E38 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

\log p_{2}\cdots\log p_{k}\leq(1.0005\log k)^{k-1}.

Accordingly, since p_{k}\geq k, for k>c_{10}

2^{k-1}h(\theta_{2})\cdots h(\theta_{k})\leq(\log p_{2}+2R)\cdots(\log p_{k}+2R)\leq(1.001\log k)^{k-1}.(53)

Furthermore as for the proof of ([39](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E39 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([40](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E40 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) we find that from ([51](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E51 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

h(\alpha_{1})\leq c_{11}h(\theta)k\log k(54)

and, from ([12](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S2.E12 "In 2. Preliminary lemmas ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([28](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E28 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([33](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E33 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

h(\theta)\log m\leq 8\log|\alpha|\log n.(55)

Thus by ([43](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E43 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([48](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E48 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([51](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E51 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([53](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E53 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), ([54](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E54 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([55](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E55 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")),

\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)<c_{12}k^{4}\log p\left(7e\left(\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)1.001\frac{k\log k}{\log p}\right)^{k}p\log|\alpha|\log n.(56)

Therefore, by ([31](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E31 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), for p>c_{13} we again obtain ([41](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S4.E41 "In Proof. ‣ 4. Further preliminaries ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and the result follows. ∎

## 5. Proof of Theorem 1

Let c_{1},c_{2},\dots denote positive numbers which are effectively computable in terms of \omega(\alpha\beta) and the discriminant of \mathbb{Q}(\alpha/\beta). Let g be the greatest common divisor of (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta. Note that \varphi(n) is even for n>2 and that

\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=g^{\varphi(n)/2}\Phi_{n}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})

where \alpha_{1}=\alpha/\sqrt{g} and \beta_{1}=\beta/\sqrt{g}. Further (\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})^{2} and \alpha_{1}\beta_{1} are coprime and plainly

P(\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta))\geq P(\Phi_{n}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})).

Therefore we may assume, without loss of generality, that (\alpha+\beta)^{2} and \alpha\beta are coprime non-zero integers.

By Lemma 7 there exists c_{1} such that if n exceeds c_{1} then

\log|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|\geq\frac{\varphi(n)}{2}\log|\alpha|.(57)

On the other hand

\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{p\mid\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)}p^{\text{ord}_{p}\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)}\qquad\qquad.(58)

If p divides \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) then, by ([4](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S1.E4 "In 1. Introduction ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")), p does not divide \alpha\beta and so

\text{ord}_{p}\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)\leq\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1),(59)

where \wp is a prime ideal of \mathcal{O}_{K} lying above p. By Lemma 1 if p divides \Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta) and p is not P(n/(3,n)) then p is at least n-1 and thus for n>c_{2}, by Lemma 8,

\text{ord}_{\wp}((\alpha/\beta)^{n}-1)<p\exp(-\log p/51.9\log\log p)\log|\alpha|\log n.(60)

Put

P_{n}=P(\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)).

Then, by ([58](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S5.E58 "In 5. Proof of Theorem 1 ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and Lemma 1,

\log|\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta)|\leq\log n+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq P_{n}\\
p\nmid n\end{subarray}}\log p\ \text{ord}_{p}\Phi_{n}(\alpha,\beta).(61)

Comparing ([57](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S5.E57 "In 5. Proof of Theorem 1 ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([61](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S5.E61 "In 5. Proof of Theorem 1 ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and using ([59](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S5.E59 "In 5. Proof of Theorem 1 ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) and ([60](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#S5.E60 "In 5. Proof of Theorem 1 ‣ On divisors of Lucas and Lehmer numbers")) we find that, for n>c_{3},

\varphi(n)\log|\alpha|<\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq P_{n}\\
p\nmid n\end{subarray}}c_{4}(\log p)p\exp(-\log p/51.9\log\log p)\log|\alpha|\log n

hence

\frac{\varphi(n)}{\log n}<(\pi(P_{n},n,1)+\pi(P_{n},n,-1))P_{n}\exp(-\log P_{n}/51.95\log\log P_{n}),

and, by Lemma 3,

c_{5}\frac{\varphi(n)}{\log n}<\frac{P^{2}_{n}}{\varphi(n)\log(P_{n}/n)}\exp(-\log P_{n}/51.95\log\log P_{n}).

Since \varphi(n)>c_{6}n/\log\log n,

P_{n}>n\exp(\log n/104\log\log n),

for n>c_{7}, as required.

## 6. Proof of Theorem 2

Since p does not divide ab

\text{ord}_{p}~(a^{n}-b^{n})=\text{ord}_{p}((a/g)^{n}-(b/g)^{n})

where g is the greatest common divisor of a and b. Thus we may assume, without loss of generality, that a and b are coprime. Put u_{n}=a^{n}-b^{n} for n=1,2,\cdots and let \ell=\ell(p) be the smallest positive integer for which p divides u_{\ell}. Certainly \ell divides p-1. Further, as in the proof of Lemma 3 of [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)], if n and m are positive integers then

(u_{n},u_{m})=u_{(n,m)}.

Thus if p divides u_{n} then p divides u_{(n,\ell)}. By the minimality of \ell we see that (n,\ell)=\ell so that \ell divides n. In particular \ell divides p-1. Furthermore, by (4), we see that

\text{ord}_{p}u_{\ell}=\text{ord}_{p}\Phi_{\ell}(a,b).

If \ell divides n then, by Lemma 2 of [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1274v1#bib.bib38)],

(u_{n}/u_{\ell},u_{\ell})~\text{divides}~n/{\ell},\\(62)

and so

\text{ord}_{p}~u_{p-1}=\text{ord}_{p}u_{\ell}(63)
  

Suppose that p divides \Phi_{n}(a,b). Then p divides u_{n} and so \ell divides n. Put n=t\ell p^{k} with (t,p)=1 and k a non-negative integer. Since \Phi_{n}(a,b) divides u_{n}/u_{n/t}~\text{for}~t>1 we see from (62), since (t,p)=1, that t=1. Thus n=\ell p{{}^{k}}. For any positive integer m

u_{mp}/u_{m}=pb^{(m-1)p}+\binom{p}{2}b^{(m-2)p}u_{m}+\cdots+u_{m}^{p-1}

and if p is not 2 and p divides u_{m} then \text{ord}_{p}(u_{mp}/u_{m})=1. It then follows that if p is an odd prime then

\text{ord}_{p}\Phi_{\ell p^{k}}(a,b)=1\qquad\text{for}~k=1,2,\cdots.

If n is a positive integer not divisible by \ell=\ell(p) then |u_{n}|_{p}=1. On the other hand if \ell divides n and p is odd then

|u_{n}|_{p}=~|u_{\ell}|_{p}\cdot|n/\ell|_{p}.(64)

It now follows from (63) and (64) and the fact that \ell\leq p-1 that if p is an odd prime and \ell divides n then

|u_{n}|_{p}=~~|u_{p-1}|_{p}\cdot|n|_{p}.(65)

Therefore if p is an odd prime and n a positive integer

\text{ord}_{p}(a^{n}-b^{n})\leq\text{ord}_{p}(a^{p-1}-b^{p-1})+\text{ord}_{p}n,(66)

and our result now follows from (66) on taking n=p-1 in Lemma 8.
