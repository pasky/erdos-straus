Then replace `RESULT_PENDING` with the actual certification totals and audit trail.

2. **major — §2.2 lines 93–94: the subgroup criterion uses the wrong modulus.**
   **Problem:** The event is not that prime-factor classes **modulo 24** generate a subgroup containing −1. It is the corresponding statement in the particular group modulo 4s, 4t, or 4C. Prime factors dividing that modulus must also be excluded. Counterexample: p=13, s=1 gives p+s=14, with divisor 7 ≡ −1 (mod 4), hence the solution (o,e,C)=(1,2,2). Modulo 24, the unit prime factor 7 generates {1,7}, which does not contain 23; the other prime factor 2 is not even a unit modulo 24.
   **Suggested fix:** State the criterion separately modulo the relevant q ∈ {4,8,12,24}, using only prime factors coprime to q. Because these groups have exponent two, any generated element can be represented using each eligible prime at most once, so this corrected subgroup criterion really is equivalent to existence of the required divisor. Alternatively work modulo 24 only with explicit projection to the relevant quotient, not the target class 23 itself. Lemma 2.2's exponent classification remains correct.

3. **major — §4.1 lines 183–184: finite counterexamples do not rule out an eventual growing non-residue bound.**
   **Problem:** In the intended character formulation, eight listed exceptions with q(p)=2 disprove a proposed implication only when its range includes those primes and f(p) ≥ 2 there. They do not show that no implication of this kind can hold for all sufficiently large p with f(p) tending to infinity. A growing f can be small on any prescribed finite set. There is also a 2-adic wording problem: literally being a quadratic residue modulo 2 imposes no restriction on odd p, whereas (2/p)=1 requires p≡±1 (mod 8), hence p≡1 (mod 8) in the present prime class. Finally, mere growth beyond a constant would not combine with Burgess/Vinogradov to finish the target: f must eventually exceed the applicable upper bound for the least non-residue, not merely tend to infinity.
   **Suggested fix:** Formulate the proposed condition as (ℓ/p)=1 for every prime ℓ≤f(p), or treat the supplementary condition modulo 8 separately from quadratic residuacity modulo odd primes. Say that the data refute a uniform characterization covering these small exceptions, but neither establish nor exclude an eventual characterization. Specify the quantitative growth needed to contradict q(p) ≪ε p^{1/(4√e)+ε}.

4. **major — §0 lines 12–16 and §2.3 lines 105–108: a correct bounded-exponent lemma is promoted into an unproved methodological impossibility.**
   **Problem:** Lemma 2.3 establishes which finite groups are exactly resolved by characters of bounded order. It does not establish that every possible pointwise averaging argument must recover every individual residue class exactly, or that a higher-order averaged/correlated argument is impossible. Thus “the only pointwise-in-p averaging tool” and “the full extent of this route” do not follow from the PROVED lemma. The generic assertion that higher reciprocity transfers arbitrary order-k characters to a setting with appropriate pointwise Burgess bounds also needs hypotheses and a precise theorem if it is to be used mathematically.
   **Suggested fix:** Label the methodological consequence Assessment, replace the universal assertions by “I found no way to extend this mechanism,” and retain the precise proved obstruction only for exact class recovery by a fixed finite collection of character orders. For a collection of orders use their least common multiple in Λ.

## Proof audit: claims that survive

**Lemmas 1.1–1.6.** The parity and two-ordering argument is correct for odd n. In Lemma 1.2 positivity of the divisor gives C≥1 and a positive t; the odd-n condition supplies the required opposite parity. In Lemma 1.3, d≡−1 implies its complementary divisor is also −1, giving the inverse construction. The two divisors cannot coincide for odd n, since that would give equal entries and hence even n; thus the factor two is exact. The assertion p∤d applies when n=p. Lemma 1.4 correctly uses the strict inequality PQ>p/(4C), including the boundary 64C³=p. Lemma 1.5 forces the multiple of p to be exactly p; opposite parity and relabeling then finish the argument for odd p.

For Lemma 1.6, there is a short independent proof, so no external barrier memo is needed. Put d=4Co−1. Then gcd(d,n)=1, n≡−o (mod d), d≡3 (mod 4), and (o/d)=1 by quadratic reciprocity and d≡−1 (mod o). For n≡1 (mod 4), reciprocity gives (d/n)=(n/d)=(−o/d)=−1. The other factor has the same Jacobi symbol since (4Co−1)(4Ce−1)≡1 (mod n). If n is an odd square both coprime Jacobi symbols must instead be +1, a contradiction. For prime n these are Legendre symbols.

**Lemmas 2.1–2.3.** All three fibration bijections and (d) are correct for the ordered surface. For m≡3 (mod 4), s≤(m+1)/4<m, and a hit implies s=(−p mod m)>0, t=(p+s)/m=ceil(p/m), and C=(m+1)/(4s). Thus p≡−s−t (mod 4st); m>√p is not actually necessary for this identity. If m divides p there is simply no hit. Lemma 2.2's classification is right: odd prime powers permit only 3 to the first power and the 2-adic exponent is at most 3. The group map in its proof is more naturally a surjection onto (Z/ℓ)× than the displayed containment, but the exponent inference is valid.

Lemma 2.3's explicit Λ(k) is correct, including the delicate endpoints. For odd k, only 2¹ is permitted in q. For even k with b=v₂(k)≥1, λ(2^a)|k permits exactly a≤b+2. For odd ℓ, the conditions are (ℓ−1)|k and vℓ(q)≤1+vℓ(k). Combining prime powers by lcm gives exactly the divisors of Λ(k). In particular Λ(1)=Λ(3)=2, Λ(2)=24, Λ(4)=240, Λ(6)=504, Λ(12)=65520.

**Theorem W, all five steps.** Step 1's forbidden residues have the correct sign. For ℓ>4K+1, the distinct positive integers 4C<ℓ have distinct nonzero inverses; none equals zero, and ω(ℓ)≤floor(K)+1<ℓ. For the discarded small primes ω=1<ℓ. Avoidance of zero uses p>Q. Complete-level inclusion is applied only after its cutoff, correctly.

Step 2 uses the standard arithmetic large-sieve upper bound with squarefree denominator sum J=Σ_{q≤Q} μ²(q)∏_{ℓ|q}ω(ℓ)/(ℓ−ω(ℓ)). An interval of integers up to x fits the stated x+Q² numerator. Replacing each local factor by g(ℓ)=ω(ℓ)/ℓ only decreases J. Step 3's fixed-modulus prime sums yield κ=1+Σ_{C≤K}1/φ(4C); overlapping progressions are counted with the correct multiplicity because they supplied distinct excluded residues.

Step 4 is valid. For g≥0 and a=ℓ^σ≥1, the exact local ratio is (1+ga)/(1+g)=1+g(a−1)/(1+g)≤1+g(a−1). With 0≤σ log ℓ≤1, a−1≤(e−1)σ log ℓ, proving the disputed product inequality. Choose B_K uniformly for z≥3, then s=max(1,log(2B_K)), then x large enough that Q^{1/s}≥3. This ordering removes any apparent circularity: B_K does not depend on s. The tail is at most Π/2. The inequality log(1+g)≥g−g²/2 and Σg(ℓ)²<∞ give Π≫K(log z)^κ and hence G(Q)≫K(log Q)^κ. Step 5 therefore gives exactly exponent 1+κ_K. The omitted O_K(x^{1/4}) primes are absorbed, and bounded x can be handled by enlarging the K-dependent constant. Finally φ(4C)≤4C gives κ_K≥H_floor(K)/4≥(log K)/4, also for real K≥1. Choosing a fixed K for each A proves the all-log-powers assertion, not the pointwise target.
