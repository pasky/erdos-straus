   The obstruction. Inclusion–exclusion over correlated classes is controlled by a cluster expansion,
and its convergence criterion needs the moduli to be Q-rough, that is, free of prime factors ≤ Q.
Roughness keeps only the fraction ≍ 1/ log Q of the mass, and the cluster sums must be bounded
with log Q small. A step-by-step bound does not allow this: it loses a power of log log N . Three
ideas resolve the difficulty.

   (i) A real Kotecký–Preiss bound (§3). For real activities, Dobrushin’s induction gives the upper
       bound we need in a few lines, without convergence of any expansion.
  (ii) Averaging over the small primes (§§5–6). We split [1, N ] into the unit classes modulo the

       sum of order log y0 , and the mass becomes ≍ L3 /K.
 (iii) Clusters in a random order (§7). The weight of a cluster factors over its graph of shared
       primes. When its vertices are summed in an order chosen for its shape, a vertex is cheap if it
       has many earlier neighbours or many later ones. In a random order the number of earlier
       neighbours is uniformly distributed, so on average every vertex is cheap.

   Where 34 comes from. The following heuristic explains the exponent. Two rates fix it. The
mass of the Type II classes with moduli up to 2L grows like L3 /K, one logarithm for each free
scale a, c, d, and Proposition 6.9 attains this order. Elsholtz and Tao [7, Theorem 1.1] show that
the average number of solutions per prime lies between (log p)3 and (log p)3 log log p (for K = 4),
so no system of Type I or II classes grows much faster. (In the terminology of [7], these are
the solutions in which n divides exactly one, respectively exactly two, of x, y, z.) The transfer
(Lemma 2.2) evaluates intersections of up to 2k ≍ µ events, and an event of modulus near 2j
uses about j of the log2 N available bits. Scale j therefore brings mass ≍ j 2 /K per unit of j at
a cost of j bits per event. The bits are best spent on all scales j ≤ L with 0L j 3 dj ≍ K log N ,
