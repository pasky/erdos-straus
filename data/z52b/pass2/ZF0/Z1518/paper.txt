                                           1         Introduction
                                           Ancient Egyptians used sums of unit fractions (whose numerators are 1) to express frac-
                                           tions due to their ways of distributing food. For instance, 5/8 was interpreted by ancient
                                           Egyptians as distributing five pancakes among eight people fairly. They cut first four
                                           pancakes into halves and cut the last one into eight equal pieces. So each person got the
                                           same share: 1/2 + 1/8 pancakes. This amazingly interpreted the mathematical equation
                                                                                         5  1 1
                                                                                           = + .                                             (1.1)
                                                                                         8  2 8
                                           So an Egyptian fraction is a sum of distinct unit fractions. By repeatedly applying the
                                           simple fact
                                                                               1      1         1
                                                                                 =        +           ,                               (1.2)
                                                                               k    k + 1 k(k + 1)
                                           one can easily prove that any fraction is a Egyptian fraction. However, it is very difficult
                                           to determine if a fraction can be expressed as a sum of fixed number of unit fractions (cf.
                                           [6] for an excellent exposition and extensive references). For example, it is difficult to know
                                           if a fraction can be written as a sum of two unit fractions (e.g., cf. [10, 11, 12, 14, 22]).
                                           In 1948, Erdös and Straus formulated a conjecture : for any positive integer n > 2, there
                                           exist positive integers n1 , n2 and n3 such that
                                                                                     4   1   1  1
                                                                                       =   +   + ,                                           (1.3)
                                                                                     n   n1 n2 n3
                                               1

which is still open up to now. For each n, the number of solution can go to infinity as n
does. Elscholtz and Tao [6] found excellent bounds for it.
     If n = 24m + 1 and n1 ≤ n2 , n3 , then n1 = 6m + k with 1 ≤ k ≤ 12m. A solution
(n1 , n2 , n3 ) of the equation (1.3) is called a tame solution if n2 and n3 are factors of
(6m + k)(24m + 1). We call n = 24m + 1 wild if it does not have any tame solution. In
[35], we derived twenty-eight families of tame solutions of the equation (1.3). Numeric
test shows that they cover all the tame primes. Based on the information in our earlier
work on tame solutions posed in arXiv, Howerton [9] found that there are only fourteen
wild primes of the form n = 24m + 1 ≤ 2.4 × 1011 . In [36]. we derived thirty-four families
of wild solutions of the equation in (1.3), which contain the solvability of the fourteen
wild primes. Together with our earlier tame polynomial solutions in [35], numeric test
shows that they cover all the primes of the form 24m + 1.
     In this article, we give a necessary and sufficient polynomial condition for a number of
the form 24m + 1 to be a prime number. Then we present the 56 solvability polynomials
consisting of our earlier tame polynomial solutions in [35], wild polynomial solutions
in [36] and the polynomials in this paper for composite numbers of the form 24m + 1.
Numeric test shows that they imply Erdös-Straus conjecture. Moreover, we give a list
of 36 exponential-polynomial functions consisting of our earlier 28 tame solutions in [35]
and the polynomials for composite numbers, whose numeric test shows that Howerton’s
conjecture on fourteen wild primes holds.
     Goldbach conjecture says that any even integer ≥ 4 is a sum of two primes, which is
still open today. As a byproduct of this work, we find a version of Goldbach conjecture
modulo 24. The paper is organized as follows.
   In Section 2, we derive a necessary and sufficient condition for a number of the form
24m+1 to be a prime. In Section 3, we present 56 solvability polynomials for Erdös-Straus
conjecture. In Section 4, we present 36 solvability exponential-polynomial functions for
Howerton’s conjecture. Moreover, we given Goldbach conjecture modulo 24 in Section 5.


2    Primes of the Form 24m + 1
In this section, we want to find the conditions that a positive integer of the form 24m + 1
is a prime.
    Let p > 3 be a prime. Then one of the following conditions must hold:

                      (1) p ≡ 1 (mod 12);        (2) p ≡ 5 (mod 12);                    (2.1)

                     (3) p ≡ 7 (mod 12);         (4) p ≡ 11 (mod 12).                   (2.2)
In Case (1), p = 12k + 1 for some k ∈ N, and

                    (12k + 1)2 = 144k 2 + 24k + 1 = 24k(6k + 1) + 1.                    (2.3)

In Case (2), p = 12k + 5 for some k ∈ N, and

 (12k + 5)2 = 144k 2 + 120k + 25 = 24(6k 2 + 5k + 1) + 1 = 24(2k + 1)(3k + 1) + 1. (2.4)

In Case (3), p = 12k + 7 for some k ∈ N, and

 (12k + 7)2 = 144k 2 + 168k + 49 = 24(6k 2 + 7k + 2) + 1 = 24(2k + 1)(3k + 2) + 1. (2.5)

In Case (4), p = 12k + 11 for some k ∈ N, and

 (12k + 11)2 = 144k 2 + 264k + 121 = 24(6k 2 + 11k + 5) + 1 = 24(k + 1)(6k + 5) + 1. (2.6)

   In summary, we have:
   Theorem 2.1 Let m ∈ N. Then n = 24m + 1 is a prime if and only the following
condition hold:
   (1) m ̸≡ s (mod 24s + 1) for 0, m ̸= s ∈ N;
   (2) m ̸≡ 5s + 1 (mod 24s + 5) for any s ∈ N;
   (3) m ̸≡ 7s + 2 (mod 24s + 7) for any s ∈ N;
   (4) m ̸≡ 11s + 5 (mod 24s + 11) for any s ∈ N;
   (5) m ̸≡ 13s + 7 (mod 24s + 13) for any s ∈ N;
   (6) m ̸≡ 17s + 12 (mod 24s + 17) for any s ∈ N;
   (7) m ̸≡ 19s + 15 (mod 24s + 19) for any s ∈ N;
   (8) m ̸≡ 23s + 22 (mod 24s + 23) for any s ∈ N.

   The sufficiency follows from the following proposition.

    Proposition 2.2. For any positive integer m, n = 24m + 1 is a composite number if
and only n = (24s+1)(24t+1) for some positive integers s and t; or n = (24s+p)(24t+p)
for some p ∈ {5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23} and s, t ∈ N.

                 m = s + t(24s + 1) = s + t + 24st        for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.            (2.29)

      m = 5s + 1 + t(24s + 5) = 1 + 5(s + t) + 24st             for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.      (2.30)

In the case that (3) in the above theorem fails, we have

      m = 7s + 2 + t(24s + 7) = 2 + 7(s + t) + 24st             for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.      (2.31)

Suppose that (4) in the above theorem fails. We have

    m = 11s + 5 + t(24s + 11) = 5 + 11(s + t) + 24st             for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.     (2.32)

If (5) in the above theorem fails, then

    m = 13s + 7 + t(24s + 13) = 7 + 13(s + t) + 24st             for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.     (2.33)

When (6) in the above theorem fails, we have

   m = 17s + 12 + t(24s + 17) = 12 + 17(s + t) + 24st              for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.   (2.34)

In the case that (7) in the above theorem fails, we have

   m = 19s + 15 + t(24s + 19) = 15 + 19(s + t) + 24st              for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.   (2.35)

Suppose that (8) in the above theorem fails. We have

   m = 23s + 22 + t(24s + 23) = 22 + 23(s + t) + 24st              for s, t ∈ N, st ̸= 9.   (2.36)

5     Goldbach Conjecture Modolo 24
In his letter to Euler 1874, Goldbach proposed the following conjecture: any even integer
≥ 4 is a sum of two primes. The most advanced progress in this direction is Chen’s
Theorem which was announced in 1966 and the detailed proof was published in 1973
(cf. [3, 4, 5]). Chen’s Theorem says that any even integer ≥ 4 is a sum of a prime
and the product of at most two primes. There are many significant works on Goldbach
conjecture (cf. [32] for excellent exposition). For instance, [7, 19, 18, 20, 28, 24, 34] are
also interesting related works.
