2. Rishika Agrawal, University of Georgia

   Writing Rational Numbers as a Sum of Reciprocals of Shifted Golomb Primes

  The study of decomposing rational numbers into sums of distinct unit fractions is one of the
  oldest topics in additive number theory. Many interesting questions arise when we restrict
  the denominators to some specific subset S of rational numbers. Bloom has proved that 1 can
  be expressed as a sum of reciprocals of shifted primes, corresponding to the set S = {p − 1 :
  p a prime}. In this talk, we will explain why the same remains true for shifted Golomb primes.
  Golomb primes is a density 0 subset of the primes constructed sequentially using the rule: qj
  is a Golomb prime if qj is not 1 mod q for all Golomb primes q ≤ qj .




3. Mohsen Aliabadi, University of California, San Diego

                          Acyclic Matchings in Abelian Groups

  The inquiry into identifying sets of monomials that can be eliminated from a generic homo-
  geneous polynomial via a linear change of coordinates was initiated by E. K. Wakeford. This
  linear algebra problem prompted C. K. Fan and J. Losonczy to introduce the notion of acyclic
  matchings in the additive group Zn , subsequently extended to abelian groups by the latter
  author. Alon, Fan, Kleitman, and Losonczy established the acyclic matching property for Zn .
  This talk aims to classify all abelian groups with respect to the acyclic matching property.
4. Ali Alsetri, University of Kentucky

                    Density Version of the Binary Goldbach Problem

  Let δ > 12 . We prove that if A is a subset of the primes such that the relative density of A
  in every reduced residue class is at least δ, then almost all even integers can be written as
  the sum of two primes in A. The constant 12 in the statement is best possible. Moreover we
  give an example to show that for any ϵ > 0 there exists a subset of the primes with relative
  density at least 1 − ϵ such that A + A misses a positive proportion of even integers.
  Joint work with Xuancheng Shao, University of Kentucky.

6. Alexander Borisov, Binghamton University

                           Locally Integer Polynomial Functions

  A locally integer polynomial function on an infinite subset of the integers is an integer-
  valued function whose restriction to every finite subset is given by a polynomial with integer
  coefficients. These functions form interesting rings, and their theory bears some mysterious
  resemblance to the theory of complex-analytic functions. In particular, they form a sheaf
  rather than just a presheaf with respect to cofinite and density one topologies on the sets of
  integers and natural numbers. Many natural open questions remain, in particular regarding
  the sheafification of their presheaf in the Kirch topology and other adic topologies. The
  talk will be based on my recent preprint: https://people.math.binghamton.edu/borisov/
  documents/papers/LIP_INTEGERS_v2.pdf




7. Walter Bridges, University of North Texas

    Integer Partitions and Statistics for Random Representations of Lie Algebras

  Choose a partition of a large integer uniformly at random. How big do we expect the largest
  part to be? How many 1’s are there? What does the Young diagram look like? For that
  matter, how can we generate large partitions efficiently, to collect data and make conjec-
  tures? Following work on partitions by Erdős, Turán, and many others, Fristedt introduced
   a statistical mechanics inspired approach to these sorts of questions that has proved widely
   useful in analytic combinatorics. I will discuss an extension of Fristedt’s methodology and
   describe how a typical, large-dimensional representation looks for the family of complex Lie
   algebras, slr+1 (C). (The case r = 1 corresponds to integer partitions.) In particular, we give
   asymptotic probability distributions for the multiplicity of small irreducible representations,
   as well as the largest dimension, the largest height, and the total number of irreducible rep-
   resentations appearing in the decomposition of a representation sampled uniformly from all
   representations with the same dimension.
   Joint work with Kathrin Bringmann, University of Cologne and Caner Nazaroglu, University
   of Cologne.




 8. Kyle Burke, Florida Southern College

                    On the Computational Complexity of Battle Sheep

   Battle Sheep is a board game published by Blue Orange, previously released under the name
   Splits. It is a normal play combinatorial game where players move stacks of tokens across a
   board of hexagons to block their opponent’s moves. We show that this game is PSPACE-hard
   via a reduction from Two Player Bounded Constraint Logic.
   Joint work with Hirotaka Ono, Nagoya University.




 9. Caroline Cashman, College of William & Mary

                                Black Hole Zeckendorf Games

   Zeckendorf proved that every positive integer can be written as a decomposition of non-
   adjacent Fibonacci numbers. Baird-Smith, Epstein, Flint and Miller converted reaching an
   integer’s Zeckendorf decomposition into a two-player game. They gave a non-constructive
   proof that Player 2 has a winning strategy for n ̸= 2; a constructive solution is unknown.
   We expand by investigating new variants of this game. The Black Hole Zeckendorf game
   on Fm is played in columns Fi for i < m. Gameplay is similar to the original Zeckendorf
   game, except any piece placed on Fi for i ≥ m is removed from play. With these constraints,
   we analyze the games with black holes on F3 and F4 and construct a solution for specific
   configurations, using a parity-stealing based non-constructive proof to lead to a constructive
   one. We also examine a pre-game in which players take turns placing down n pieces on the
   outermost columns before the decomposition phase, and find constructive solutions for any
   n.
   Joint work with Steven Miller, Williams College; Jenna Shuffelton, Williams College, and
   Daeyoung Son, Williams College.

14. Alfie Davies, Memorial University of Newfoundland

                        Comparisons in the Misère Blocking Universe

   We present a finite comparison test for short games modulo the misère blocking universe,
   and also an alternative characterisation for invertible blocking games. The blocking universe
   was recently introduced as a means to study pocancellativity. Roughly speaking, a game is
   “blocking” if, whenever a player runs out of moves and the other player gives moves back
   to them, then the original player can move to a position where they again have no moves.
   Finite comparison tests have been established for many other universes, but it is not fully
   understood when such a test exists, although it is known that some universes cannot exhibit
   one.
   Joint work with Rebecca Milley, Grenfell Campus, Memorial University of Newfoundland.

18. Ben Doyle, Purdue University

                    Lp -means of Exponential Sums over r-Frees in Fq [t]

   The moments of exponential sums which are restricted to a certain subset of the integers can
   reveal interesting additive information about these sets. With this in mind, Balog and Ruzsa,
   and later Keil, examined the k-th moments of exponential sums over the r-free integers,
   which are integers not divisible by r-th powers. In particular, Keil found the exact order of
   magnitude of these moments for all k > 0, with the exception of the case k = 1 + 1/r, where
   he missed by a factor of log(X). We examine the analogous problem in the function field
   setting, obtaining a result analogous to Keil’s, with two added improvements. In the case
   k = 1 + 1/r, we obtain the exact order of magnitude, and in the case k > 1 + 1/r, we refine
   the result to an asymptotic formula using the Hardy-Littlewood circle method.




19. Dennis Eichhorn, UC Irvine

              Open Problems Involving Cranks For Partition Congruences

   Dyson famously conjectured, correctly, that his rank statistic witnesses Ramanujan’s first two
   congruences for p(n), and that there exists a “crank” statistic that witnesses Ramanujan’s
   congruence modulo 11 in a similar fashion. As it turns out, this phenomenon of congruence-
   witnessing statistics, which we now also call “cranks” in homage to Dyson, also occurs in other
   contexts within partition theory. In this talk, we give several open problems and conjectures
   in this area, highlighting some recent developments along the way.
   This talk will include joint work with several coauthors.




20. Steve Fan, Department of Mathematics, University of Georgia

                               Counting Shifted-Prime Divisors

   For every positive integer n, let ω ∗ (n) denote the number of positive divisors of n which
   are shifted primes, namely, numbers of the form p − 1 with p prime. The study of ω ∗ was
   initiated by Prachar in 1954, who showed, among other things, that the values of ω ∗ (n) can
   be abnormally large on rare occasions, and was recently picked up by Murty and Murty and
   by Ding, Guo and Zhang with a focus on its first two moments. Remarkably, this function
   and its variants have played important roles in the development of the first unconditional
   deterministic primality test, running in nearly polynomial time, by Adleman, Pomerance and
   Rumely in 1983, and in the proof of the infinitude of Carmichael numbers discovered in the
   seminal 1994 work by Alford, Granville, and Pomerance. Despite previous work on ω ∗ , its
   precise distribution has remained a mystery. In this talk, I will discuss my recent work, both
   jointly with Pomerance and independently, on the distribution of ω ∗ .
   Joint work with Carl Pomerance, Department of Mathematics, Dartmouth College.




21. Sohail Farhangi, University of Adam Mickiewicz

     Undecidability in the Ramsey Theory of Polynomial Equations and Hilbert’s
                                   10th Problem

   A central topic of study in arithmetic Ramsey theory is to determine for a given integral
   domain R which polynomials p ∈SR[x1 , · · · , xn ] are partition regular, i.e., when is it the case
   that for any finite partition R = ℓi=1 Ci , there exists some 1 ≤ i0 ≤ ℓ for which Ci0 contains a
   solution to p(x1 , · · · , xn ) = 0. We reduce this question to a variant of Hilbert’s 10th problem
   over R, and thereby determine the exact lightface complexity of the set of partition regular
   polynomials for many integral domains R. We will then discuss the combinatorial implications
   of this set theoretic result. Time permitting, we will discuss analogous results in the realm
   of density Ramsey theory. In this setting, we were required to prove a compactness principle
   and a uniformity principle of independent interest for countable cancellative left amenable
   semigroups.
   Joint work with Bill Mance, University of Adam Mickiewicz and Steve Jackson, University
   of North Texas.




22. Michael Filaseta, University of South Carolina

               A Solution to a 1973 Conjecture of P. Erdős and J. Selfridge

   A covering system is a collection of congruences x ≡ aj (mod mj ) such that every integer
   satisfies at least one of the congruences. It is called distinct if the mj are distinct. Let C be
   a finite distinct covering system. L. Mirsky and D. J. Newman showed that if the minimum
   modulus m0 of C exceeds 1, then the sum of the reciprocals of the moduli in C exceeds 1.
   In a 1952 paper of P. Erdős, he mentions that H. Davenport questioned whether a stronger
   condition on the minimum modulus, like m0 > 2, would lead to a larger lower bound than
   1. It is now well-known that the sum of the reciprocals of the moduli in C can be arbitrarily
   close to 1 if m0 ∈ {2, 3, 4}. In a 1973 paper of Erdős, he mentions the conjecture, attributed
   to himself and J. Selfridge, that if m0 > 4, then the sum of the reciprocals of the moduli in C
   is greater than c for some fixed c > 1. In this talk, I will describe further history and recent
   joint work with Alexandros Kalogirou which resolves this conjecture of Erdős and Selfridge.
   Joint work with Alexandros Kalogirou, University of South Carolina.

27. Robert (Scotty) Groth, University of South Carolina

                   New Irreducibility Criteria on Legendre Polynomials

   Legendre polynomials, where the nth Legendre polynomial, written Pn (x), is of degree n, are
   a classical family of polynomials that first arose in A. M. Legendre’s study of the forces of
   attraction between spheroids. It has long been conjectured that P n (x) := Pn (x) is irreducible
   (over Q) for n ≥ 2 even and P n (x) := Pn (x)/x is irreducible (over Q) for n ≥ 3 odd. Using
   Newton polygons, along with other techniques, we present an algorithm to determine if P n (x)
   is irreducible for a given n. We then use this algorithm to deduce the irreducibility of P n (x)
   for 2 ≤ n ≤ 107 . Using similar ideas, we produce several new irreducibility criteria for P n (x)
   that build on prior results. Most notably, in an extension of existing irreducibility criteria,
   we show, for a fixed odd prime q, that Pp+q (x) is irreducible for every prime p that is not
   in some finite exceptional computable set of primes Pq . We demonstrate the computational
   aspects of this result by showing Pp+q (x) is irreducible for every odd prime q ≤ 13 and every
   prime p, and we obtain some additional partial results for q ≤ 29.

32. Brad Isaacson, New York City College of Technology (CUNY)

39. Jeffrey C. Lagarias, University of Michigan

                               Generalized Factorials, Extended

   Many generalizations of the factorial function and of binomial coefficients have been studied.
   We consider a class of such factorials in the spirit of Knuth and Wilf (1989), that are built
   of products of “generalized integers.” In 1997 Manjul Bhargava introduced a (multiplicative)
   generalization of the factorial function, first for Z, then for Dedekind domains. Given an
   arbitrary subset S of the ring Z, the generalized factorials associated to S can be used to
   describe the ring of polynomials that are integer-valued on the set S. These factorials are
   specified by their prime factorization, and determining the power of p dividing the k-th
   factorial associated to S is done by means of “p-sequence” invariants of S. This talk will
   describe extensions to define “b-sequence” invariants associated to every ideal (b) of Z, giving
   rise to a larger class of generalized factorials. These results have a further extension to all
   ideals of Dedekind domains. The talk discusses properties of these factorials.
   Joint work with Wijit Yangjit, University of Michigan.




40. Daniel Larsen, Massachusetts Institute of Technology

              Carmichael Numbers in All Possible Arithmetic Progressions
   In 1994, Alford, Granville, and Pomerance introduced a method for producing infinitely many
   Carmichael numbers. Their method was then adapted by Matomäki and subsequently Wright

            Algorithms to Write Integers as the Sum of Consecutive Primes

   Let f (n) denote the number of representations a positive integer n has as a sum of consecutive
   primes. For example, f (17) = 2 since 17 is prime and 17 = 2 + 3 + 5 + 7, with no other
   representations. In 1963, Moser showed that the average value of f (n) is asymptotically log
   2. Moser also posed four questions on f (n):
    (a) Is f (n) = 1 infinitely often?
    (b) Is f (n) = k solvable for every k?
    (c) Does the set of integers n for which f (n) = k have positive density?
    (d) Is the lim sup f (n) = ∞?
   One can also find these questions in Guy’s Unsolved Problems in Number Theory. In this
   talk, we will describe and analyze a couple of algorithms that compute the histogram h(x, k),
   the number of n ≤ x such that f (n) = k. We will also give conjectured answers to Moser’s
   four questions based on data from our histogram for x = 1012 .

86. Francesca Yu, Carnegie Mellon University

                           Structure-Biased Maker-Breaker Games

   In classical Maker-Breaker games on graphs, Maker and Breaker take turns claiming edges;
   Maker’s goal is to claim all of some structure (e.g., a spanning tree, Hamilton cycle, etc.),
   while Breaker aims to stop her. The standard question considered is how powerful a Breaker
   Maker can defeat; i.e., for the 1 : b biased game where Breaker takes b edges per turn, how
   large can b be for Maker to still have a winning strategy, for various possible goal sets? We
   introduce a variant of this question in which Breaker is required to choose their multiples edges
   as the edges of (a subgraph of) a given structure (e.g., a matching, clique, etc.) on each turn.
   We establish the order of magnitude of the threshold biases for Triangle Games, Connectivity
   Games, and Hamiltonicity Games under clique, matching, and star biases respectively. We
   conclude that in most cases structure imposes major obstruction to Breaker, opening up a set
   of games whose strategies deviate from the classical biased Maker-Breaker game strategies,
   and shedding light on the types of Breaker strategies that will and will not work to prove
   tighter bounds in the classical setting.
   Joint work with Wesley Pegden, Carnegie Mellon University.
