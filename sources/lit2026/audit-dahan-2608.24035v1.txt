                                           1     Introduction
                                           Formulated by Paul Erdős and Ernst G. Straus in 1948 [2], the Erdős–Straus conjecture posits that
                                           for every integer n ≥ 2 there are positive integers x, y, z with
                                                                                         4  1 1 1
                                                                                           = + + .                                                     (1)
                                                                                         n  x y z

                                               A decomposition of 4/p for a prime p | n extends to n, so the conjecture reduces to prime n;
                                           and the polynomial identities and local congruence methods going back to Mordell [5] settle every
                                           residue class of primes except those with n ≡ r2 (mod 840), r ∈ {1, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23}. All six of
                                           these lie in the class n ≡ 1 (mod 24), which is the class considered throughout. For n even, for
                                           n ≡ 3 (mod 4), and for n ≡ 1 (mod 24) with (n + 3)/4 divisible by 5, elementary identities of the
                                           same vintage apply; we do not reproduce them. The third of these is a sub-case of the class studied
                                           here, dispatched by Theorem 2.3 at the shift c = 3 with d1 = K3 and d2 = K3 /5.

                                           1.1    Context and related work
                                           Three methodological strands situate what follows. Vaughan [1] established the density bound
                                           O(N exp(−cV (log N )2/3 )) for an absolute constant cV > 0, for the exceptional set, a strong existence

                                                                                                   1
guarantee which does not analyse any explicit search procedure. Computational verifications have
reached n ≤ 1014 (Swett [6]), 1017 (Salez [7]) and 1018 (Mihnea and Dumitru [11]) by exhaustive
search, giving numerical evidence at large bounds without asymptotic guarantees on density or cost.
The present paper sits between the two: it analyses an explicit factorization-free procedure and
certifies the depth at which it succeeds, at the price of a density bound weaker than Vaughan’s.
    Several recent preprints propose constructive frameworks for sub-cases of the conjecture: three-
dimensional affine lattices [12], “tame” solutions [13], a cubic surface model [14], parametric identities
for related conjectures [15, 16, 17], and the Type A/B/C classification of [23, 10]. Section 5 shows
that all of them are projections of the single parameter space K = (n + c)/4, and records what
is due to whom; we claim no priority for the characterisations themselves. The analytic frame is
Vaughan’s, with the Type I/Type II dichotomy in the form of Elsholtz–Tao [8] and Pomerance–
Weingartner [19], whose closed criteria we use throughout; for the Schinzel generalization the latter
show that the threshold nm , if it exists, satisfies nm ≥ exp(m1/3+o(1) ). The closest antecedents
for the exceptional-set arguments of Section 4.2 are Sander [21, 22], who applied the Rosser and
half-dimensional sieves to this equation. Ventas [18] explores the finiteness of counterexamples via
continued fractions.

1.3   Outline
Section 2 sets up the divisor-based condition, the complete criterion at a fixed shift with its two
branches, the Euclidean transfer identity, the search-space bound and Algorithm 1. Section 3 spe-
cialises to n ≡ 1 (mod 24): the filter via divisors of n + 1, the hyperbolic reformulation and its
exact position inside Type II, the two-parameter criterion, and the two factorization-free procedures.
Section 4 contains the analytic results: the exact sieve dimension at fixed depth (Section 4.1), the
two-sided blind set (Section 4.2), the depth/density trade-off at growing depth (Section 4.3), and
a discussion with a heuristic model for the shape of the curve (Section 4.4). Section 5 relates the
construction to the recent literature, Section 6 reports the numerical verifications item by item, and
Section 7 lists what is left open.

2.7   The deterministic bounded search algorithm
Algorithm 1 searches the family of Theorem 2.3 by scanning δ at each shift. It should not be
advertised as a competitive decision procedure: a segmented sieve of the interval (n/4, 3n/4] followed
by Lemma 2.2 decides the Erdős–Straus property of a single n in Õ(n) operations, which is better
than the worst-case bound of Corollary 2.17 below. Its interest is structural: it searches a family
strictly larger than the hyperbolic one, it requires no factorization, and the number of shifts it rejects
before succeeding is very small in practice: over all 3202 primes n ≡ 1 (mod 24) below 3 · 105 the
smallest admissible shift never exceeded c = 63 (Section 6). It is in this role, as a cheap complement
resolving the blind spot H of Proposition 3.8, that we retain it.
     Two points about the pseudo-code. Arithmetic is counted at unit cost. The exit test on the
modulator is a heuristic accelerator only: at a rejected step the quantity a(δ) = σ(δ)/ gcd(K, V (δ))
is not the modulator of any solution, and a later δ with a large gcd could in principle produce a
smaller a together with an admissible pair. We have not excluded this and have observed no instance
of it (Section 6: over 250 primes and the shifts c ≤ 31, the accelerator never skipped a pair admissible
for Theorem 2.3). The unconditional guarantee is the test V > K 2 of Theorem 2.16, which alone
ensures that no admissible δ is skipped. Theorem 2.16 and Corollary 2.17 are accordingly stated for
the variant in which the accelerator is disabled; the accelerated version as printed carries no proof
of completeness, only the empirical evidence of Section 6. Note also that the correct quantity in
that test is m = V /d2 and not K/d2 : by Theorem 2.12 the modulator is a = (q + 4 − b)/(4m) with
m = K/d1 = V /d2 , and at the point where the test is reached one has d2 ∤ K, so that K/d2 is not
even an integer.

3.6     A complete factorization-free batch procedure on [N, 2N ]
The same inversion applies verbatim to the two branches of Corollary 2.7, and then yields a complete
decision procedure for every prime of the interval, at essentially the same cost on one half of the
dichotomy and at a quadratic cost on the other. Locating that asymmetry is the point of this
subsection.
    We use (4) and (5), both congruence conditions on n modulo an explicit modulus and so both
sievable: n has a Type II solution iff there are u, v, e ≥ 1 with e | u + v and n ≡ −e (mod 4uv),
where 4uv ≤ 2n [19, (3.4)], so uv ≤ N for n ≤ 2N , and e ≤ u + v; and n has a Type I solution iff
there are u, d, f ≥ 1 with f | 4u2 d + 1 and n ≡ −f (mod 4ud), where 4ud ≤ 2n + 1 [19, Lemma 7.4],
so ud ≤ N + 1, and f ≤ 35 n.
    Two apparent obstacles are removed by enumerating a cofactor rather than a divisor. The first is
the enumeration of the divisors e of u + v: writing a = (u + v)/e for the modulator and enumerating
(u, a, e) instead of (u, v) together with the divisors of u+v, so that v := ea−u, visits every admissible
triple exactly once, with no divisor list and no wasted step. The second is the enumeration of the
divisors f of 4u2 d + 1: for fixed u and fixed e with gcd(e, 2u) = 1, the condition e | 4u2 d + 1 is
the single congruence d ≡ −(4u2 )−1 (mod e), so the admissible d form one arithmetic progression
modulo e, and f = (4u2 d+1)/e follows by one division. No factorization and no divisor enumeration
is ever performed; the only non-trivial operation is one extended Euclidean algorithm per pair (u, e),
which we count at unit cost along with the rest of the arithmetic.

Discussion. In substance, Algorithm 3 is the scheme underlying the large-scale verifications of
Salez [7] and of Mihnea and Dumitru [11]; we claim no novelty for the verification itself, only for
the explicit statement of its complexity and for the observation that it is factorization-free. What
Theorem 3.17 does and does not establish should be stated precisely. It does not make the interval
problem quasi-linear: at Θ(N 2 ) it is no better than the natural alternative, which runs the batch
sieve of Proposition 3.16 at cost O(N log2 N ) and then treats the residual set prime by prime, for
a total of N 2−o(1) , since every known upper bound for the exceptional set, including Corollary 4.31
and Vaughan’s N exp(−cV (log N )2/3 ), is of the shape N 1−o(1) , and the per-prime cost is at best
n1−o(1) . Nor does it buy anything for a single prime, for which the same criteria still require Θ(n)
markings. What it does buy is the location of the obstruction: the Type II half of the problem is
genuinely quasi-linear on an interval, while the entire quadratic cost sits in the Type I half, where
the divisors of 4u2 d + 1 must be produced without factorization. That asymmetry between the two
halves of the Elsholtz–Tao dichotomy is, to our knowledge, not recorded elsewhere.
    The quadratic cost is not an artefact of a careless schedule. Let M = 4u2 d+1 with d ≤ (N +1)/u,
so M ≤ 4u(N + 1) + 1; any factorisation M = ef has e ≤ E or f ≤ 4u(N + 1)/E, since otherwise
ef > 4u(N + 1) ≥ M − 1. A procedure which, for each u separately, produces the divisor pairs of
the progression {4u2 d + 1}d by scanning one side must therefore√scan a range of length at least E
on one side and 4uN/E on the other, √   at a cost ≫ E + 4uN/E ≫ uN by the arithmetic–geometric
mean inequality, minimised at E = 2 uN , which is the choice made above; summing over u ≤ N
returns ≫ N 2 . This is an argument about the scanning schedule of Algorithm 3 and not a lower
bound in any formal model of computation. Whether a sub-quadratic factorization-free procedure
exists by some other route is left open.

5     Relation to recent parametrizations
The local parametrizations discussed here appeared as independent preprints between late 2025 and
mid-2026; we claim no priority over them. The object of this section is a retroactive unification:
each is a restricted sub-variety or an algebraic projection of the parameter space K = (n + c)/4.

5.1   Three recent constructions as sub-varieties of (K, c)
The tame sub-variety (modulator 1). For primes n = 24m+1, Xu [13] uses a first denominator
n1 = 6m + k and defines a tame solution by the requirement that the numerator summands S1 , S2 ,
with S1 + S2 = 4k − 1, be positive integers; his Theorem 2.1 shows that for n prime this forces S1
and S2 to divide 6m + k separately.
Proposition 5.1. The class of tame solutions is the sub-variety of Theorem 2.3 on which the mod-
ulator equals 1.
Proof. Equating the first denominator K with n1 gives K = 6m+k, and n = 4K −c with n = 24m+1
yields c = 4(6m+k)−(24m+1) = 4k −1. By Xu’s Theorem 2.1 the summands divide K, so d1 = S1
and d2 = S2 are divisors of K with d1 + d2 = 4k − 1 = c; the relation d1 + d2 = ac then forces a = 1,
and conversely a = 1 returns a tame solution.

    This is strictly weaker than condition (iii) of Theorem 3.6, which demands uv | K rather than
u | K and v | K separately: the hyperbolic family is contained in the tame one, which is why the
set H of Proposition 3.8 contains Xu’s wild set a priori.

Affine lattice projections. Dyachenko [12] studies the identity (4B − 1)(4C − 1) = 4nδ + 1 in
variables (B, C, δ).
Proposition 5.2. That affine lattice projects onto the (K, c) parameter space by a rational change
of variables, the generated denominators being recovered from it.
Proof. Expanding gives 16BC − 4B − 4C = 4nδ, i.e. 4BC − (B + C) = nδ. Dividing by δ and
substituting K = BC/δ, c = (B + C)/δ recovers 4K − c = n. Setting σ = B, the denominators of
                                                   nσK
Lemma 2.1 evaluate to K = BC/δ, σn = Bn and cσ−K         = Cn. Exploring the affine lattice therefore
amounts to localized divisor searches within the (K, c) space. We claim only this direction; we have
not verified that every (K, c) with the required divisor structure arises from an integral triple, so
the correspondence is stated as a projection and not as a bijection.

Cubic surfaces. Bello-Hernández, Benito and Fernández [14] define a divisor function fab (n, ·, ·) =
k with first denominator (n + k)/4 and map decompositions to the surface P (X, Y, Z) = XY Z −
X − Y = n.
Proposition 5.3. The parameter k maps to the shift c, and the surface P (X, Y, Z) = n is the
resolvent of Theorem 3.3 under substitution.
Proof. Equating the first denominator (n + k)/4 with K = (n + c)/4 identifies k with c, and the
requirement k ≡ 3 (mod 4) matches the shift constraint. Substituting X = u, Y = v, Z = 4α into
XY Z − X − Y = n yields 4αuv − u − v = n, which is Theorem 3.3.

5.2     Lopez’s Types A, B and C
The framework of Lopez [23, 10] is the closest to ours. Lopez classifies the solutions of a prime
p = 4κ + 1 by their shape rather than by the congruence which produces them. Type A has shape
(du, dv, duv), characterised [23] by the existence of t ≥ 0 and a divisor w | κ + 1 + t with w ≡ −1
(mod 3 + 4t). Type B has shape (duv, dup, dvp), characterised [10, Thm. 4 and Thm. 6] by the
existence of t ≥ 0 and u, v with uv | κ + 1 + t and u + v = 3 + 4t. Type C has shape (uv, uwp, vwp),
characterised by −p ≡ 4d2 (mod 4dm − 1). All three are Type II in the sense of Definition 2.8 when
p is prime.

Proposition 5.4. Write c = 3 + 4t, so that κ + 1 + t = Kc = (p + c)/4. Then:

 (a) Lopez’s Type A condition is Theorem 2.3 restricted to d1 = 1: a divisor w = d2 | Kc with
     1 + w ≡ 0 (mod c).

    (b) Lopez’s Type B condition is condition (iii) of Theorem 3.6, i.e. exactly the hyperbolic family
        of Section 3.2 and the search space of Algorithm 2.

    (c) Consequently the family of Theorem 2.3 contains both Types A and B, at every shift c ≡ 3
        (mod 4) with 0 < c < 4n. In particular, Lopez’s Conjecture 1, that every prime has a Type A
        or a Type B solution, implies that the untwisted branch (2) is never empty.

Proof. (a) is the substitution c = 3 + 4t in w ≡ −1 (mod 3 + 4t); (b) is Theorem 3.6(iii) verbatim
after the same substitution; (c) is immediate, both conditions being instances of “∃ d1 , d2 | Kc with
c | d1 + d2 ”, which is the hypothesis of Theorem 2.3 on its whole range 0 < c < 4n.

    The containment in (c) is a statement about the family of Theorem 2.3, not about Algorithm 1.
The latter scans only c ≤ n − 1, under the standing hypothesis c < n of Section 1.4 which the
Euclidean tracking of Section 2.6 requires, whereas admissible shifts run to c ≤ 2n by Proposition 2.5
and Lopez’s parameter t = (c − 3)/4 is not bounded a priori. Algorithm 1 therefore reaches a Type
A or Type B solution only when the corresponding shift satisfies c < n, which is the second of the
two limitations recorded in Section 2.3.
    Proposition 5.4 means that Theorem 3.4 is a reformulation of a characterisation already published
by Lopez, and that Theorem 2.3 generalises his Type A criterion by allowing d1 > 1. We claim no
priority on these points. What is added here is the delimitation of the hyperbolic family inside the
Type I/Type II classification (Theorem 3.6, Corollary 3.7), the extension and structural explanation
of the blind spot (Proposition 3.8 and Corollary 4.5, priority for the list of nine belonging to Xu),
and the analytic results of Section 4. Our computations are consistent with Lopez’s reported data,
which is a useful external check: he notes that 193 and 2521 possess no Type A solution but do
possess Type B solutions, and that 23929 and 83449 possess no Type B solution; the latter two
belong to H, as they must, and the former two do not. Finally, Lopez [10, Cor. 3] derives from
Mordell’s restriction a Legendre-symbol condition for his Type C solutions, namely that −p be a
quadratic residue modulo 4u − 1 for some u. Theorem 4.6 is of the same nature but is attached to
the shift c rather than to a modulus 4dm − 1, and Theorem 4.7 identifies which branch it constrains;
the two statements are complementary rather than equivalent.

7    Conclusion and open questions
Four questions are left open by the analysis above, in decreasing order of tractability.
    The constant in Lemma 4.22. The bound W (J) ≥ (κ1 + o(1))(log J)2 with κ1 = 3/(2π 2 ) comes
from the crude estimate φ(m) ≤ m; the true limit of W (J)/(log J)2 appears numerically to be
close to 0.46 (Section 6, item (8)). Proving W (J) ≥ (0.45 + o(1))(log J)2 , a finite computation, by
the method of Lemma 4.21 extended to two variables, would improve every constant of Section 4.3,
taking the exponent of Corollary 4.31 from 0.065 to about 0.093. Since the saturation argument
makes only the lower bound relevant, no matching upper bound is needed.
    The τ3 -type bound for arbitrary witness systems. Proposition 4.32 pins the exponent 2/3 for
the two witness systems    exhibited here. Whether every system attached to the Type I/Type II
dichotomy satisfies p≤Z ϱ(p)/p ≪ (log Z)2 , which is what a genuine impossibility statement for
                     P
this method would require, is not proved.
    A sub-quadratic factorization-free interval procedure. Theorem 3.17 locates the whole quadratic
cost of a complete decision on [N, 2N ] in the extraction of the divisors of 4u2 d + 1 in the Type I
pass. Whether T a sub-quadratic factorization-free procedure exists by some other route is left open.
    Whether J E2 (N ; J) is empty. By Theorem 3.9 this is exactly the question of whether every
prime n ≡ 1 (mod 24) has a Type II solution. Theorem 4.3 shows that the intersection has counting
function ≪A N (log N )−A for every A, and Corollary 4.5 gives the same for the one-parameter

analogue H, whose fourteen known members all disappear at depth 5 once the modulator is free;
but emptiness is a different matter, and the general conjecture remains open.
