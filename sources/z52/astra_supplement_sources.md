## Pell equation: universal solvability, but logarithmically many bounded solutions

**Theorem (Pell).** For every positive nonsquare integer \(D\), the equation
\[
x^2-Dy^2=1
\]
has a solution in positive integers \(x,y\). The continued-fraction expansion of \(\sqrt D\) is periodic; convergents at the appropriate period endpoint give the fundamental positive solution \((x_1,y_1)\). Every positive solution is generated by
\[
x_n+y_n\sqrt D=(x_1+y_1\sqrt D)^n,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
\]
Thus, for fixed \(D\), the number of solutions with \(x,y\le H\) is \(O_D(\log H)\): the left side grows geometrically in \(n\), with ratio \(x_1+y_1\sqrt D>1\). This logarithmic height count is a consequence of the power parametrization, not a uniform-in-\(D\) bound; the fundamental solution can be very large.

**Identifiable authored exposition accessed:** Hendrik W. Lenstra, Jr., “Solving the Pell equation,” in *Algorithmic Number Theory: lattices, number fields, curves and cryptography*, MSRI Publications 44, Cambridge University Press (2008), accessed 24-page copy, §1, pp. 1–3 (book page range not verified). The copy states an earlier version appeared in Notices AMS 49 (2002), 182–192. PDF: https://library.slmath.org/books/Book44/files/01lenstra.pdf. Accessed 2026-10-30. Lenstra explicitly gives the power formula for the ordered solutions, identifies the first as the fundamental solution, demonstrates the continued-fraction method, and describes exponential growth of \(x_n,y_n\). The article says the continued fraction of \(\lfloor\sqrt d\rfloor+\sqrt d\) is purely periodic and the period endpoint yields the fundamental solution (with two periods used in the odd-period case). The displayed \(O_D(\log H)\) bound follows directly from these statements.

## Dirichlet approximation and Minkowski volume forcing

**Dirichlet (one-dimensional approximation).** For every real \(\theta\) and integer \(N>0\), there are integers \(h,k\) with \(0<k\le N\) and \(|k\theta-h|<1/N\). This is a pigeonhole existence theorem with an explicit denominator bound; it does not assert few possible approximants.

**Source accessed:** Lawrence C. Paulson, “Formalising an easy proof: Dirichlet’s approximation theorem,” 2025-01-22, https://lawrencecpaulson.github.io/2025/01/22/Dirichlet_Approx.html (accessed 2026-10-30). The exposition states the theorem with exactly the above quantifiers and strict inequality, and gives the pigeonhole proof. It identifies its textbook source as Tom M. Apostol, *Modular Functions and Dirichlet Series in Number Theory*, p. 143. Paulson’s post links the formal Isabelle theory; the post is the accessed authored exposition.

**Minkowski convex-body theorem.** If \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^n\) is a full-rank lattice and \(K\subset\mathbb R^n\) is convex, centrally symmetric about zero, and has volume greater than \(2^n\det(\Lambda)\), then \(K\) contains a nonzero point of \(\Lambda\). (The standard closed-body formulation also allows volume \(\ge 2^n\det(\Lambda)\) under the usual compact/convex-body hypotheses.) This is a volume threshold forcing at least one lattice witness, not a small-count assertion; enlarging a body or the determinant threshold changes coordinate bounds.

**Identifiable authored reference accessed:** J. S. Milne, *Algebraic Number Theory*, course notes, University of Michigan, version 3.08 (2017), https://www.math.mcgill.ca/darmon/courses/18-19/algebra2/milne.pdf (accessed 2026-10-30). This is an author-maintained exposition covering Minkowski’s theorem in its geometry-of-numbers treatment. The statement above is the standard convex-body formulation; no original Minkowski page or exact theorem number was independently verified in this pass. For the classical publication record, Hermann Minkowski, “Geometrie der Zahlen,” Teubner, 1910; the book itself was not accessed. **Do not represent Minkowski’s original theorem/page as directly checked.**

## Sperner’s lemma and Brouwer: finite combinatorics versus a continuous conclusion

**Sperner lemma (simplex form).** Triangulate an \(n\)-simplex with vertices labeled \(0,\ldots,n\), subject to the boundary rule that a vertex on a face may only receive a label belonging to that face. Then the number of small \(n\)-simplices whose vertices carry all \(n+1\) labels is odd (in particular, nonzero). For any fixed finite triangulation this is a finite, discrete witness: a fully labeled small simplex.

**Brouwer fixed-point theorem (simplex form).** Every continuous map from a compact simplex to itself has a fixed point. The Sperner-based proof applies the lemma on successively finer triangulations; compactness gives a convergent subsequence and continuity yields an actual fixed point in the limit. So the combinatorial lemma supplies finite witnesses at each mesh scale, whereas the fixed-point conclusion is a continuous/limit statement; there is no implication that one fixed finite triangulation itself contains an exact fixed point.

**Identifiable authored exposition accessed:** Nikolai V. Ivanov, “Sperner’s lemma, Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem, and cohomology,” arXiv:0906.5193v2 (current version dated 2019-08-24; first version 2009-06-29), https://arxiv.org/html/0906.5193v2 (accessed 2026-10-30). The article states Sperner’s lemma as odd parity of fully labeled simplices, states Brouwer for a simplex, and proves the latter from finer subdivisions, compactness, and continuity. It also lists Emanuel Sperner, “Neuer Beweis für die Invarianz der Dimensionszahl und des Gebietes,” *Abhandlungen aus dem Mathematischen Seminar der Universität Hamburg* 6 (1928), 265–272, and Knaster–Kuratowski–Mazurkiewicz, *Fundamenta Mathematicae* 14 (1929), 132–137. These original papers were not accessed.

## Four squares, three squares, and the actual count scale

**Lagrange four-square theorem.** Every nonnegative integer is a sum of four integer squares (equivalently, every positive integer is so representable; zero is represented by \((0,0,0,0)\)).

**Jacobi’s four-square formula and scale.** Let \(r_4(n)\) count ordered integer quadruples \((a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb Z^4\) with \(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2=n\). For every positive integer \(n\),
\[
r_4(n)=8\sum_{d\mid n,\;4\nmid d}d.
\]
In particular, for every odd prime \(p\), \(r_4(p)=8(1+p)\), so the number of integer witnesses is \(\Theta(p)\) along the primes—not a uniformly bounded or logarithmic witness count. This exact prime case is enough to show the scale can grow linearly in the input. (The formula also immediately implies Lagrange’s existence theorem.)

**Source accessed:** George E. Andrews, Shalosh B. Ekhad, and Doron Zeilberger, “A Short Proof of Jacobi’s Formula for the Number of Representations of an Integer as a Sum of Four Squares,” arXiv:math/9206203v1, submitted 1992-06-03; published in *The American Mathematical Monthly* 100 (1993), 273–276. arXiv record: https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9206203; full text: https://arxiv.org/html/math/9206203v1 (accessed 2026-10-30). The abstract explicitly identifies Jacobi’s representation-count formula and says it implies Lagrange’s theorem. The formula above uses the standard convention counting ordered signed integer representations; the prime specialization is direct substitution.

**Legendre three-square theorem (including exceptions).** A positive integer \(n\) is a sum of three integer squares if and only if it is not of the form
\[
4^a(8b+7),\qquad a,b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.
\]
The excluded shape is necessary by residues modulo 8 and repeated division by 4, and the theorem asserts sufficiency for every other positive integer. In particular, the exceptions are exactly—not merely a subset of—those integers of the displayed form.

**Identifiable authored source accessed:** Paul Pollack, “Dirichlet’s proof of the three-square theorem,” *Mathematics of Computation* 88 (2019), 2259–2271, DOI 10.1090/mcom/3376; publisher page/PDF: https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/2019-88-316/S0025-5718-2018-03349-X/viewer/ (accessed 2026-10-30). The abstract states the Gauss–Legendre criterion with precisely the excluded form \(4^k(8m+7)\), for nonnegative integers. It discusses Dirichlet’s proof. No three-square representation-count formula is asserted here; the verified count scale above is specifically Jacobi’s four-square formula.
